## experiment 1: regret curves

**what people usually predict:** UCB and Thompson sampling well ahead of everything else, with ε-greedy in between and greedy last; most people expect UCB, the algorithm with the logarithmic guarantee, to beat any ε-greedy.

**what happens:** after 3,000 rounds the average regrets are greedy **362.5**, ε-greedy (0.01) **167.3**, UCB ($c = 1$) **99.9**, ε-greedy (0.1) **82.3**, Thompson **45.0**. greedy's curve is a straight line on the linear plot: **75%** of its runs still add more than 50 regret in the last 1,000 rounds (they are stuck on a bad arm for good). ε-greedy (0.1) also ends on a straight line, adding 17.8 in the last 1,000 rounds, close to its exploration tax $\varepsilon\bar\Delta = 0.1 \times 0.15 = 0.015$ per round (the average gap over all five arms is 0.15). ε = 0.01 explores too little: 28% of its runs are still on a bad arm at the end. UCB is *behind* ε-greedy (0.1) at this horizon, though its curve is bending over. Thompson's curve is nearly flat by the end (3.5 added in the last 1,000 rounds). the slider shows how sensitive UCB is to $c$: with $c = 0.5$ it drops to about 41, as good as Thompson; with $c = 2$ (the default) it's above 200.

**why:** greedy is linear because a bad first impression is never corrected (on paper 2): it stops collecting data on the arm it underestimates. ε-greedy fixes that but pays $\varepsilon$ times the average gap per round forever, so it's linear with a small slope; with a tiny $\varepsilon$ the slope is tinier but it takes so long to explore that many runs haven't found the best arm by $T = 3{,}000$. UCB's bound is $O(\sum_a\ln T/\Delta_a)$, and the constant matters: with gaps as small as 0.05–0.1, the bonus $c\sqrt{\ln t/N_a}$ has to shrink below $\Delta_a$, which takes $c^2\ln t/\Delta_a^2$ pulls, hundreds per arm. an asymptotic rate says nothing about who's ahead at $T = 3{,}000$: ε-greedy (0.1) wins early, UCB wins eventually. Thompson sampling explores in proportion to the posterior probability of being best, which shrinks fast for clearly bad arms, so it wastes the fewest pulls.

## experiment 2: which gap is hardest?

**what people usually predict:** the smaller the gap, the harder the problem and the bigger the regret (the Lai–Robbins constant grows like $1/\Delta$).

**what happens:** regret is small at *both* ends and peaks in the middle. for Thompson: **3.7** at $\Delta = 0.005$, rising to **17.9** at $\Delta \approx 0.065$, falling to **2.8** at $\Delta = 0.45$. UCB has the same shape, a little higher everywhere (peak about 25). on the left, the measured regret follows the $\Delta T$ line (both arms are played about equally); on the right, it falls with the $(\Delta/\mathrm{KL})\ln T$ line.

**why:** regret is gap × pulls of the bad arm. with a tiny gap you can't tell the arms apart in 2,000 rounds, so you play each about half the time, but each bad pull costs only $\Delta$: regret $\approx \Delta T/2$, small. with a big gap you identify the best arm in a handful of pulls: regret $\approx \ln T/\Delta$, also small. the worst case is where the two meet, where telling the arms apart takes about the whole horizon: $T \approx 1/\Delta^2$, i.e. $\Delta \approx 1/\sqrt T \approx 0.02$ here (the measured peak is a few times larger, because "telling apart" needs a few standard errors). that's why the worst-case (minimax) regret of bandits is $\Theta(\sqrt{kT})$, while the regret on any *fixed* problem is $O(\ln T)$: the hard problem moves as $T$ grows. the Lai–Robbins line blows up as $\Delta \to 0$ because it's an asymptotic statement for fixed $\Delta$ and $T \to \infty$; at a fixed horizon it can't exceed $\Delta T$.

## experiment 3: beating the lower bound?

**what people usually predict:** that the measured regret sits on or above $C\ln t$, since it's a lower bound.

**what happens:** over 100 runs, Thompson's average regret at 20,000 rounds is **20.1 ± 1.2** (mean ± standard error), about 40% of $C\ln T = 49.4$, and the average curve stays below the line the whole way. the slope of regret against $\ln t$ is **3.24 ± 0.33** over rounds 200–2,000 and **3.24 ± 0.27** over 2,000–20,000: steady, at about two thirds of $C = 4.98$. (with only 20 runs the slopes look as if they fall; that's noise: the per-run slopes vary a lot, which is why the lab now averages 100 runs and prints standard errors.)

**why:** there's no contradiction. the theorem bounds $\liminf_{T\to\infty}\bar R_T/\ln T$: it says that *eventually* the regret must grow at least as fast as $C\ln T$, up to lower-order terms, and for any finite $T$ those terms can be large and negative. the measured curve is roughly a straight line in $\ln t$ with a smaller slope and an offset; the theory says the slope must creep up to $C$, but only as $\ln T$ grows without bound, so slowly that 20,000 rounds don't show it. the bound counts the pulls needed to be *confident at level $1/T$* that the worse arm is worse: $\ln T/\mathrm{KL}(0.45, 0.55) = 9.9/0.020 \approx 490$ pulls, while Thompson needed about $20.1/0.1 \approx 200$. most runs separate the two arms well before that worst-case confidence level is reached, and a Bayesian algorithm with a sensible prior exploits that. the practical lesson is the same as in experiment 1: asymptotic results tell you which algorithms are sound (sublinear, the right order), but for a fixed budget the constants and lower-order terms decide, and you have to measure, with error bars.
