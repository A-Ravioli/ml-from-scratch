# monte carlo methods

## why this matters

dynamic programming needs the model: every transition probability $P(s' \mid s, a)$ as a number. try writing that down for blackjack. the probability of going from "13 with a usable ace, dealer shows a 2" to "19, no usable ace" after a hit is computable, but the probability of each *final* outcome when you stick depends on the whole distribution of the dealer's hidden card and every card the dealer will draw. it's a page of combinatorics for a toy card game, and for anything real (a robot, a market, a video game) nobody can write $P$ down at all.

but simulating blackjack is trivial: deal cards, play, see who won. **Monte Carlo** (MC) methods learn from exactly that: complete episodes of experience, with no model. the idea is the one you used in chapter 01 to estimate integrals: a value is an expected return, so estimate it by averaging returns you observed. that turns policy evaluation into averaging and policy improvement into generalized policy iteration with sampled values. it also opens a door DP never needed: learning about one policy (the one you want) from episodes generated by another (the one you can afford to run), through importance sampling. that's the first **off-policy** method in this course, and the variance problems you'll see in its lab follow off-policy RL all the way to ch 24.

## core ideas

### idea 1: blackjack, and learning from episodes

in Sutton & Barto's blackjack (Example 5.1), you play against a dealer with an infinite deck: every card is drawn independently, each of the 13 ranks with probability $1/13$, face cards counting 10 and an ace 1 or 11. you get two cards, the dealer two (one face up). you **hit** (take a card) until you **stick** or go over 21 (**bust**, you lose). once you stick, the dealer hits until reaching 17 or more (including a "soft" 17 with an ace counted as 11), then whoever is closer to 21 wins: $+1$, $-1$, or 0 for a draw; a dealer bust is a win for you. an ace that can count as 11 without busting is a **usable ace**. your state is (your sum, the dealer's face-up card, whether you hold a usable ace): 18 sums × 10 cards × 2 = 360 states. we skip the casino's special payout for naturals (an initial 21 is just a 21), which keeps the state Markov.

notice what kind of environment this is. there's no `P` array, and episodes are short (one to a few decisions) and *always end*. that's the setting Monte Carlo methods need: they wait until an episode is over, then use the actual return $G_t$ from every step of it. no bootstrapping from estimates of other states, no model.

> **spec.** `Blackjack()` subclasses `Env`: `observation_space = Discrete(360)`, `action_space = Discrete(2)` (0 stick, 1 hit). static methods: `encode(player_sum, dealer_card, usable_ace)` $= (\text{player\_sum} - 4)\cdot20 + (\text{dealer\_card} - 1)\cdot2 + \text{usable\_ace}$; `decode(obs)` returns `(player_sum, dealer_card, usable_ace)` as `(int, int, bool)`; `hand_value(cards)` returns `(total, usable)`, counting one ace as 11 when that doesn't exceed 21. `draw()` returns `min(int(self.np_random.integers(1, 14)), 10)`. the hands live in lists `self.player` and `self.dealer` (`dealer[0]` is face up). `reset` calls `Env.reset`, then deals player, player, dealer, dealer, and returns `(obs, {})`. `step(1)` draws a card: a bust returns reward $-1$ and `terminated=True` (the obs clips the sum to 21), otherwise reward 0. `step(0)` lets the dealer draw while their total is below 17, then scores $+1$/0/$-1$ with `terminated=True`. `truncated` is always False; invalid actions raise `ValueError`; `observe()` returns the obs for the current hands.

> **▶ build: stage 1.** implement `Blackjack`.

### idea 2: Monte Carlo prediction

$V^\pi(s) = \mathbb E_\pi[G_t \mid S_t = s]$ is an expectation, so estimate it by an average. run episodes with $\pi$; every time the episode passes through $s$, the return that followed is one sample of $G_t$ given $S_t = s$. two variants:

- **first-visit MC** averages, per episode, only the return after the *first* visit to $s$. each episode contributes one sample per state, the samples are independent, and the estimate is unbiased with variance shrinking like $1/n$.
- **every-visit MC** averages the returns after *every* visit. the samples within an episode are correlated and the estimate is biased for finite $n$, but it's consistent too and often has lower error in practice (Singh & Sutton, 1996).

(warm-up 1: first-visit says 6, every-visit says 4.5.) compute returns backwards through the episode, $G \leftarrow \gamma G + R_{t+1}$ from the last step to the first, and fold each one into a running mean, $N(s) \leftarrow N(s) + 1$, $V(s) \leftarrow V(s) + (G - V(s))/N(s)$: the same incremental mean as topic 01's bandit estimates.

three properties make MC different from DP. the estimate for one state doesn't depend on the estimates for others (no bootstrapping), so errors don't propagate. the cost per state is independent of the size of the state space, so you can estimate the values of only the states you care about. and you need episodes that end.

you'll also need the exploration rule from topic 01 as a function: `epsilon_greedy(q_values, epsilon, rng=...)` picks a uniformly random action with probability $\varepsilon$, otherwise a greedy one, with random tie-breaking.

> **spec.** `epsilon_greedy(q_values, epsilon, *, rng=None)`: `rng = as_rng(rng)`; if `rng.random() < epsilon` return `int(rng.integers(n_actions))`, else a uniformly random action among the maxima (`rng.choice`). `mc_prediction(env, policy, n_episodes, *, gamma=1.0, first_visit=True, rng=None)`: `policy` is a callable obs → action; $V$ and the counts start at zeros of size `env.observation_space.n`; an episode ends when `terminated or truncated`. **every learner from here on seeds its environment from `rng` once**: the first episode calls `env.reset(seed=int(rng.integers(2**31)))`, later episodes `env.reset()`. returns $V$.

> **🔮 predict:** the random policy on topic 02's $1\times3$ corridor has $V = (-12, -8, 0)$. averaging 3,000 episodes, how close will the MC estimate of $V(0)$ be: within 0.01, 0.3, or 3?

### idea 3: Monte Carlo control

to improve a policy without a model, you need action values: improving from $V$ requires $Q(s, a) = R(s, a) + \gamma\sum P(s'\mid s, a)V(s')$, which needs $P$. so MC control estimates $Q(s, a)$ directly, by averaging the returns that followed each (state, action) pair, and improves greedily with respect to it: generalized policy iteration with sampled evaluation.

there's a catch (warm-up 2). if the policy is greedy, it never takes the actions it currently thinks are worse, so their $Q$ estimates never improve, and a wrong belief never gets corrected: topic 01's greedy failure, now in every state. there are two classic fixes. **exploring starts** begins each episode with a random state and action; that's impossible in most real environments. **ε-soft policies** keep every action's probability at least $\varepsilon/|\mathcal A|$: act ε-greedily with respect to $Q$, both to generate the episode and as the policy being improved. this is **on-policy** control: the policy you evaluate is the one you behave with. it converges to the best ε-soft policy, not the best policy, but the greedy policy for the learned $Q$ is usually close to optimal, and you can shrink $\varepsilon$ over time.

the update after each episode: walk backwards, $G \leftarrow \gamma G + R_{t+1}$; at the first visit of each pair $(S_t, A_t)$ (or every visit), $N \leftarrow N + 1$, $Q \leftarrow Q + (G - Q)/N$. the next episode's ε-greedy choices use the updated $Q$: evaluation and improvement interleave episode by episode.

> **spec.** `mc_control(env, n_episodes, *, gamma=1.0, epsilon=0.1, first_visit=True, rng=None)`: $Q$ and the counts are zeros of shape `(n_states, n_actions)`; actions come from `epsilon_greedy(Q[obs], epsilon, rng=rng)`; env seeding as in stage 2. returns `(Q, episode_returns)`, the second a list with each episode's undiscounted sum of rewards (as floats).

> **▶ build: stage 3.** implement `mc_control`.

### idea 4: off-policy prediction with importance sampling

on-policy learning ties what you learn to how you behave. often you want them separate: learn the greedy policy while behaving exploratively, learn from logged data someone else's policy produced, or evaluate a risky new policy without running it. that's **off-policy** learning: a **target policy** $\pi$ you want to evaluate, and a **behavior policy** $b$ that generated the data. it needs **coverage**: $b(a\mid s) > 0$ wherever $\pi(a\mid s) > 0$.

the tool is importance sampling. a trajectory's probability under each policy is a product of the policies' action probabilities and the environment's transition probabilities, and the transition probabilities cancel in the ratio:

$$\rho_{t:T-1} = \prod_{k=t}^{T-1}\frac{\pi(A_k\mid S_k)}{b(A_k\mid S_k)},\qquad \mathbb E_b\big[\rho_{t:T-1}G_t \mid S_t = s\big] = V^\pi(s).$$

no model required. for an action value $Q^\pi(s, a)$ the first action is given, so the ratio starts one step later, at $t + 1$. two ways to average:

- **ordinary IS**: $\hat Q = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i\rho_iG_i$, over all $n$ visits. every term $\rho_iG_i$ has exactly the right expectation, so with first visits the average is unbiased (the every-visit version you'll build averages correlated terms from the same episode, so like every-visit MC it's biased for finite $n$, but consistent). but $\rho$ is a product of ratios and its variance can grow exponentially with the episode length; it can even be infinite (warm-up 5, and the lab).
- **weighted IS**: $\hat Q = \frac{\sum_i\rho_iG_i}{\sum_i\rho_i}$. biased (with one sample it's just that sample's $G$, whatever $\rho$ is), but consistent, and its variance is bounded when returns are: in practice it's almost always the one to use.

with a deterministic target policy, $\rho = 0$ as soon as $b$ takes an action $\pi$ wouldn't (warm-up 4: one that matched $\pi$ for 10 coin-flip decisions gets weight $2^{10} = 1024$). the incremental forms (Sutton & Barto §5.6) walk the episode backwards keeping $G$ and the running weight $W$: weighted, $C \leftarrow C + W$ and $Q \leftarrow Q + \frac{W}{C}(G - Q)$, then $W \leftarrow W\,\pi/b$, and you may stop the backward pass once $W = 0$ (nothing earlier can change). ordinary, $N \leftarrow N + 1$ and $Q \leftarrow Q + \frac{1}{N}(WG - Q)$, and **zero-weight visits still count**: they're real samples of $\rho G$ that happen to be 0.

> **spec.** `off_policy_mc_prediction(env, target_policy, behavior_policy, n_episodes, *, gamma=1.0, weighted=True, rng=None)`: both policies are $(S, A)$ probability arrays; behaviour actions are drawn with `int(rng.choice(n_actions, p=behavior_policy[obs]))`; env seeding as before; every-visit, with the ratio for $(S_t, A_t)$ covering the actions after $t$. returns $Q$ $(S, A)$.

> **🔮 predict:** a target policy that always takes action 0, a uniform behaviour policy, and an environment where the target always earns $+1$. what does weighted IS estimate after a handful of episodes? ordinary IS?

> **▶ build: stage 4.** implement `off_policy_mc_prediction`.

## worked example

**first versus every visit.** an episode visits states $0 \to 1 \to 0 \to$ end with rewards $1, 2, 3$. backwards with $\gamma = 1$: $G_2 = 3$ (from the second visit to 0), $G_1 = 5$ (from state 1), $G_0 = 6$ (from the first visit to 0). first-visit: $V(0) = 6$, $V(1) = 5$. every-visit: $V(0) = (6 + 3)/2 = 4.5$. with $\gamma = 0.5$: $G_2 = 3$, $G_1 = 2 + 0.5\cdot3 = 3.5$, $G_0 = 1 + 0.5\cdot3.5 = 2.75$.

**importance sampling.** a two-step environment: from state 0, any action leads to state 1 (reward 0); in state 1, action 0 ends with $+1$, action 1 ends with 0. the target policy always takes action 0, the behaviour policy is uniform. so $Q^\pi(0, a) = 1$ for both $a$, $Q^\pi(1, 0) = 1$, $Q^\pi(1, 1) = 0$. for a visit to $(0, a)$, the ratio covers the action at state 1: $\rho = \pi/b = 1/0.5 = 2$ if the behaviour took action 0 there (and $G = 1$), $\rho = 0$ if it took action 1 (and $G = 0$). ordinary IS averages $\rho G \in \{2, 0\}$, about half each: $\approx 1$, noisily. weighted IS computes $\sum\rho G/\sum\rho = 2k/2k = 1$ exactly, from the first useful episode on. skip the zero-weight visits in ordinary IS and you'd average only the 2s: an estimate of 2.

**blackjack's deal.** with the scripted cards ace, 6 (you), 10, 9 (dealer): your hand is a soft 17, obs = `encode(17, 10, True)`. hit a 5: $1 + 6 + 5 = 12$, and the ace can no longer be 11 without busting, so obs = `encode(12, 10, False)`. your code must reproduce all of these.

## on paper

1. **first-visit MC is unbiased.** show that the first-visit estimate of $V^\pi(s)$ from $n$ episodes that visit $s$ is an average of $n$ independent samples with mean $V^\pi(s)$. why doesn't the same argument work for every-visit?

<details><summary>solution</summary>

by the Markov property, what happens after the first visit to $s$ is distributed like a fresh episode started in $s$, independently of what happened before; so the first-visit return in each episode has expectation $V^\pi(s)$, and different episodes are independent. with every visit, the returns from later visits in the same episode are nested inside the first one's (the first includes the second), so they're correlated, and how many there are depends on the trajectory: the average over a random number of correlated terms is biased for finite $n$.

</details>

2. **the importance ratio.** show that $P_\pi(\text{trajectory})/P_b(\text{trajectory}) = \prod_k\pi(A_k\mid S_k)/b(A_k\mid S_k)$, given the same starting state.

<details><summary>solution</summary>

$P_\pi(A_t, S_{t+1}, A_{t+1}, \dots, S_T \mid S_t) = \prod_{k=t}^{T-1}\pi(A_k\mid S_k)P(S_{k+1}\mid S_k, A_k)$, and the same with $b$. in the ratio every $P(S_{k+1}\mid S_k, A_k)$ appears once on top and once at the bottom.

</details>

3. **infinite variance.** a one-state MDP: action "left" returns to the state with probability 0.9 (reward 0) or ends with reward $+1$ (probability 0.1); "right" ends with reward 0. the target always goes left ($V^\pi = 1$); the behaviour is uniform. show that $\mathbb E_b[(\rho G)^2] = \infty$ for the estimate of $V^\pi$.

<details><summary>solution</summary>

only all-left episodes have $\rho \ne 0$, and only those ending in the $+1$ have $G \ne 0$. an episode of $k$ lefts ending in success has probability $(0.5\cdot0.9)^{k-1}\cdot0.5\cdot0.1$ under $b$, ratio $\rho = 2^k$, $G = 1$. so $\mathbb E[(\rho G)^2] = \sum_{k\ge1}0.05\cdot0.45^{k-1}\cdot4^k = 0.2\sum_{k\ge1}1.8^{k-1} = \infty$. the mean $\mathbb E[\rho G] = \sum 0.05\cdot0.45^{k-1}2^k = 0.1\sum0.9^{k-1} = 1$ is finite: unbiased, infinite variance.

</details>

4. **weighted IS with one sample.** what does weighted IS estimate after a single visit with $\rho > 0$? after a single visit with $\rho = 0$?

<details><summary>solution</summary>

with $\rho > 0$: $\rho G/\rho = G$, the observed return, whatever $\rho$ is. that is the bias: a single return is not reweighted at all, so the estimate starts out as a sample of what the behaviour policy experienced, and only the relative weights of many samples correct it towards $V^\pi$. with $\rho = 0$: $0/0$, undefined; the incremental form simply doesn't update, leaving the initial value (0 here).

</details>

## check your understanding

1. MC methods don't bootstrap. name one advantage and one disadvantage of that.

<details><summary>answer</summary>

advantage: no dependence on other states' (possibly wrong) estimates, so no bias from bootstrapping and robustness to non-Markov observations. disadvantage: you must wait until the episode ends, the return's variance grows with episode length, and it doesn't work for continuing tasks without artificial episodes.

</details>

2. why does model-free control estimate $Q$ rather than $V$?

<details><summary>answer</summary>

improving a policy from $V$ needs a one-step lookahead $R(s, a) + \gamma\sum_{s'}P(s'\mid s, a)V(s')$, i.e. the model. with $Q$, the greedy action is just $\arg\max_aQ(s, a)$.

</details>

3. (the predict callout in idea 2.) how close does MC get to $V(0) = -12$ on the corridor after 3,000 episodes?

<details><summary>answer</summary>

the return from state 0 has a standard deviation of about 10 (random walks are skewed), so with about 3,000 first visits the standard error is about $10/\sqrt{3000} \approx 0.2$: within a few tenths, not 0.01. the check allows 1.2.

</details>

4. your ε-greedy MC control on blackjack reports an average return of about $-0.09$ during training. the greedy policy for its $Q$ is worth about $-0.05$. why the gap?

<details><summary>answer</summary>

the training returns come from the ε-soft *behaviour*: 10% of decisions are random (hitting on 20, sticking on 8), each costing money. the greedy policy for the learned $Q$ drops those exploratory mistakes.

</details>

5. off-policy MC with a greedy target and an ε-greedy behaviour only learns from the tails of episodes. why?

<details><summary>answer</summary>

$\rho$ becomes 0 at the last time the behaviour took a non-greedy action, so only the steps after it carry weight. in long episodes with frequent exploration, most steps learn nothing: off-policy MC control is very slow, which is one motivation for Q-learning (topic 05).

</details>

6. (the predict callout in idea 4.) why is weighted IS exactly right here, while ordinary IS is merely right on average?

<details><summary>answer</summary>

every episode with $\rho > 0$ has $G = 1$, so the weighted average of returns is 1 regardless of the weights. ordinary IS averages $\rho G \in \{0, 2\}$, which is 1 only on average.

</details>

## common bugs

- **forward returns.** symptom: wrong values for every state but the first. compute $G$ backwards from the end of the episode.
- **first-visit check by state only in control.** symptom: wrong $Q$ when a state repeats with different actions. first visits are of the *pair* $(s, a)$.
- **reseeding the env every episode from the same seed.** symptom: every episode is identical and learning stalls. seed once, on the first reset.
- **including the first action in $Q$'s ratio.** symptom: action-value estimates scaled by $1/b(a\mid s)$. the ratio for $Q(S_t, A_t)$ starts at $t + 1$.
- **breaking out of ordinary IS when $W = 0$.** symptom: estimates biased upward (2 instead of 1 in the worked example). zero-weight visits still count in the denominator.
- **no coverage.** symptom: division by zero or NaN. $b(a\mid s)$ must be positive wherever $\pi(a\mid s)$ is.
