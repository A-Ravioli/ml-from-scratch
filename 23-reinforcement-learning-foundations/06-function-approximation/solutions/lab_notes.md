# function approximation · lab notes

## experiment 1: driving up the mountain

**what people usually predict:** with $\varepsilon = 0$ the agent never explores and never finds the top; or, if it does, the largest step size learns fastest.

**what happens:** every setting except the smallest step size learns to reach the top. averaged over 2 runs, the mean steps per episode over episodes 1–50 are 200 ($\alpha = 0.1$), 196 ($0.3$), 189 ($0.5$) and 182 ($0.9$); over the last 20 episodes they are **188**, **146**, **139** and **143**. the curves sit at 200 (the time limit) for the first 10–40 episodes, then drop. the learned cost-to-go is highest (about 90 steps) around position $-0.5$ at low speed, the bottom of the valley at rest, and falls towards 0 near the goal; the corners of the map (fast cars far from the top, unreachable from the start) are still at the initial value 0.

**why:** every step costs $-1$, so the true action values are around $-100$, and the initial $\hat q = 0$ is wildly optimistic. whenever the agent tries an action in a region, that action's value drops towards the truth, so the *other* actions, still at 0, look better next time: the agent systematically tries everything everywhere, including the counterintuitive "reverse first". that's exploration by optimism, and it needs no randomness. the first episodes all hit the time limit because the agent is busy disappointing itself region by region; once enough of the valley has realistic values, the energy-pumping behaviour emerges. the cost-to-go has its peak at the bottom of the valley at rest because that's the state that needs the most swinging. the corners are unvisited, so their values never left 0. $\alpha = 0.1$ moves values only 10% of the way per visit and is still exploring after 150 episodes; $\alpha = 0.9$ learns fast early but its values are noisier, so it ends no better than $0.5$ (Sutton & Barto's Figure 10.2 shows the same ordering with more runs).

## experiment 2: coarse, fine, or both?

**what people usually predict:** the fine grid (64 tiles) is at least as good as anything else with 64 features, and the coarse grid is worst.

**what happens:** test RMS errors are **0.220** for 1 × 8 tiles, **0.559** for 1 × 64 tiles, and **0.127** for 8 tilings × 8 tiles. the fine grid's prediction is full of holes: on **57%** of the test points it has never seen a sample in that tile, and predicts 0. the coarse grid is a staircase with 8 steps.

**why:** the single fine grid has resolution but no generalization: a sample only updates its own tile, 1/64 of the interval wide, and 40 samples can't cover 64 tiles. the coarse grid generalizes broadly (each sample updates an eighth of the interval) but can only represent 8 levels. eight offset tilings of 8 tiles have the same 64 features as the fine grid but behave completely differently: each sample updates 8 overlapping tiles, so its influence reaches about one coarse tile width on either side (a tent, on paper 1), while the boundaries of the 8 tilings are staggered, so the fit can change every 1/64 of the interval. resolution and generalization are separate knobs: tile width sets generalization, the number of tilings sets resolution. with 4,000 samples the fine grid would fill its holes and catch up; tile coding's advantage is largest when data is scarce, which in RL it always is early on.

## experiment 3: the deadly triad

**what people usually predict:** that a deterministic, noise-free update on a problem whose exact solution is representable must converge, perhaps slowly.

**what happens:** with $\gamma = 0.99$, off-policy linear TD drives $\|w\|$ from **10.3** to **$6.9\times10^6$** in 5,000 expected updates, exponentially. the on-policy weighting stays bounded (**8.85**). the tabular version is bounded too (**29.5**) but converging very slowly. the smallest real part of $A$'s eigenvalues explains all three: **−0.239** (off-policy linear), **0** (on-policy linear: a singular but non-negative direction), **+0.0014** (tabular). with the slider, off-policy linear TD diverges only for $\gamma$ above about **0.883**.

**why:** the expected update is the linear iteration $w \leftarrow w + \alpha(b - Aw)$, which shrinks the error along every eigenvector of $A$ whose eigenvalue has a positive real part, and blows it up along any with a negative real part, no matter how small $\alpha$ is. with the on-policy distribution, $A$ is positive semidefinite (Tsitsiklis & Van Roy, 1997); with tabular features it's diagonally dominant (on paper 3); with linear features *and* an off-policy distribution *and* bootstrapping, neither argument applies, and in Baird's example one eigenvalue is negative. take away any one leg of the triad and the instability disappears. the tabular run's slowness is the eigenvalue $+0.0014 = (1 - \gamma)/7$: the only force pulling the values to zero is the discount, applied to one state in seven. a smaller $\gamma$ shrinks the bootstrapped term $\gamma P\Phi w$, which is what produces the negative eigenvalue; below $\gamma \approx 0.883$ it's no longer strong enough. DQN has all three ingredients (a neural network, Q-learning's bootstrapped target, replayed off-policy data); its target network and replay buffer slow the feedback loop that Baird's example exposes, and van Hasselt et al. (2018) measured how often it still diverges.
