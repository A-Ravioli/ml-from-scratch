# R2 · research milestone: reproduce a generative modeling paper

*after chapter 22 · about 40–60 hours · L5 (open): a question, a protocol and a budget; no starter code, no checks*

## why this milestone

generative modeling is where "it works" is hardest to pin down. a classifier has an accuracy; a generator has samples that look plausible, a likelihood that may disagree with the samples, and an FID that depends on the feature network, the number of samples, the reference set and the sampler. chapters 21 and 22 kept running into this: two halves of the MNIST test set scoring an FID of 123 at 50 samples, a PixelCNN with the best likelihood and the second-worst FID, a guidance scale that improves every sample's class confidence while making the distribution worse, a sampler that shrinks a Gaussian even with a perfect network. a published generative result is a claim about a specific combination of model, training budget, sampler and metric, and reproducing it forces you to identify which of those the claim really depends on.

you'll pick one paper, identify one or two of its central empirical claims, rebuild the experiment at a scale that fits a laptop, and write a reproducibility report. it's the second of three milestones (R1 after chapter 18, R3 after chapter 24) and a warm-up for the thesis of chapter 30. "the claim held", "it held only partly" and "it didn't hold at this scale, and here's my best explanation" are all successful outcomes; an unclear report is not.

## the protocol

this milestone follows the reproduction protocol of chapter 30 · topic 02, summarized in [R1](R1.md) (read it there if you haven't; the same steps, plus a metric step, 5 below). the generative-specific points:

1. **read the paper three times**, and on the third pass write down the *evaluation* protocol exactly: which metric (FID, Inception score, precision/recall, bits/dim), on how many samples, against which reference set (train or test, how many images), with which sampler and how many network calls, with or without an EMA of the weights, and with which guidance scale. these are where most "irreproducible" generative results actually differ.
2. **write the claims table before writing code**, with concrete success criteria. for generative claims the criterion is usually a *comparison* (method A beats B at equal budget; quality saturates at $n$ sampler steps; reflow lowers few-step FID), because absolute numbers won't transfer (point 5). fix a margin per claim before running: *reproduced* means the 90% interval for the effect lies inside the band the claim predicts (the paper's value ± that margin, or above a stated minimum effect when only the direction can carry over to your scale); an interval that merely excludes 0 confirms the direction, not the size (chapter 30 · topic 02 defines the verdicts); for a comparison, the margin is on the difference.
3. **list the gaps**: every unreported detail (warmup, EMA decay, gradient clipping, data augmentation, clipping of $\hat x_0$ in the sampler, the exact noise schedule, the feature network) and your choice with a reason.
4. **argue the scale-down**: what must be preserved for the claim to be tested (the sampler's step count for a few-step claim; the coupling for a flow-straightness claim; the ratio of model capacity to data for a likelihood claim) and what can shrink (resolution, width, training steps, number of samples for FID, within limits you state).
5. **choose and calibrate your metric.** this course has no Inception network: chapter 21's `FeatureNet` (via `cached_feature_net`) gives *course-internal* FIDs, comparable within your report and with nothing published. either reproduce the paper's claims as comparisons in that feature space, or download an Inception network yourself (e.g. `pytorch-fid`), say so, and still expect your absolute FIDs to differ from the paper's because of scale. always report: the number of samples, the FID of real images against the reference set at that same number (the floor), and two independent sample draws (the sampling noise).
6. **implement from the paper**, using your `mlfs` modules (`mlfs.diffusion`, `mlfs.generative`, `mlfs.ode`, `mlfs.transformer`, `mlfs.torchnn`, `mlfs.stats`). you may read the authors' code only *after* your first complete run, to diagnose a discrepancy; record every change you made because of it.
7. **sanity checks before the real runs**: the samplers against closed forms on Gaussian data (the checks of topics 03–05 are your template: an exact noise predictor or velocity must reproduce $\mathcal{N}(\mu, s^2)$); a 2-D toy (8 gaussians or two moons) where your method must cover every mode; a tiny dataset the model must memorize; and a run that reproduces bit for bit from its seed.
8. **run the experiment** with a fixed budget and **at least 3 training seeds per configuration** (generative training is expensive; 5 if a run takes under 15 minutes). log per-step losses, the EMA weights, wall-clock time, configuration and seed.
9. **analyse**: metrics with bootstrap intervals over samples *and* the spread over training seeds; the paper's comparison as a difference with an interval, judged against the margin from step 2; sample grids for every configuration (fixed noise and labels across configurations, so differences are visible). no claim stronger than its interval.
10. **report**, following the template in *deliverables*.

## choosing a paper

choose a paper whose central claim survives being scaled down, on data you have: CIFAR-10 and MNIST are cached by `mlfs.datasets`, and 2-D toys (`eight_gaussians`, `make_moons`, `make_swiss_roll`) cost nothing. anything else (CelebA at 32 × 32, ImageNet 32 × 32) you download yourself, once, and document. good choices, roughly from most to least compute-friendly:

| paper | claim to reproduce | small-scale setting | notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Song & Ermon (2019), NCSN | Langevin with the exact score gets mixture weights wrong; annealing fixes them; annealed Langevin generates digits | the 2-D mixture of topic 02's lab, then MNIST with a small noise-conditional U-Net | the toy part is cheap; the MNIST part tests the noise ladder ($\sigma_1$, $L$, $\epsilon$, steps per level) |
| Song, Meng & Ermon (2021), DDIM | DDIM with 20–50 steps nearly matches 1,000-step DDPM; quality degrades gracefully as $\eta$ goes from 0 to 1 at few steps | one DDPM trained on MNIST or CIFAR-10 at 32 × 32 (the capstone's model), sampled with every combination of $\eta \in \{0, 0.2, 0.5, 1\}$ and 10–1,000 steps | almost all the compute is sampling; a strong, cheap first choice |
| Ho, Jain & Abbeel (2020), DDPM | the simplified $\epsilon$-loss beats the weighted variational bound and $\mu$-prediction on sample quality, and the variational bound gives better likelihoods | CIFAR-10 at 32 × 32 (or MNIST), a small `TimeUNet`, three objectives at an equal budget, course FID and bits/dim | bits/dim needs the discrete decoder term or the PF-ODE likelihood (topic 04); state which |
| Nichol & Dhariwal (2021), improved DDPM | the cosine schedule improves likelihood over the linear one, most at low resolution; learned variances allow fewer sampling steps | MNIST or CIFAR-10, linear vs cosine schedule at equal budget; bits/dim and FID at 25–1,000 steps | the learned-variance part needs the hybrid loss; the schedule part alone is a clean claim |
| Ho & Salimans (2021), classifier-free guidance | FID is U-shaped in the guidance scale while Inception score (or class accuracy) rises monotonically | the capstone's conditional models, $w$ from 0 to 8 on a fine grid, 3 training seeds | the capstone already measures one slice; the reproduction needs seeds and a finer grid |
| Liu, Gong & Liu (2023), rectified flow | reflow straightens trajectories and improves one- and few-step sample quality | 2-D toys and MNIST: 1-rectified vs 2-rectified (and 3-) flows, Euler with 1–100 steps, straightness | topic 05's lab is the pilot; measure the distribution cost of reflow too |
| Lipman et al. (2023), flow matching | flow matching with OT (straight) paths trains faster and samples in fewer NFE than diffusion paths at equal budget | CIFAR-10 at 32 × 32 or MNIST: one network, linear-path flow matching vs a VP-diffusion path, FID against NFE with an adaptive or fixed-step solver | the capstone compares DDPM and flow matching once; the reproduction adds seeds and the NFE curve |
| Tong et al. (2024), minibatch OT flow matching | OT couplings lower straightness and NFE needed on 2-D benchmarks | 8 gaussians → moons, 2-D source and target, independent vs minibatch-OT pairs via `flow_matching_loss(x0=...)` | overlaps with the optimal-transport elective; its exact OT solver is the hard part |
| Karras, Aittala, Aila & Laine (2022), EDM | a second-order (Heun) sampler with their time discretization reaches a given FID in far fewer NFE than Euler/DDIM | samplers only, on a DDPM you trained (MNIST or CIFAR-10); rewrite the PF-ODE in the $\sigma$-parametrization of topic 04 · on paper 4 | no training beyond one model; the discretization ($\rho = 7$) is the interesting part |
| Peebles & Xie (2023), DiT | FID improves with model Gflops (smaller patches at fixed size), and adaLN-Zero beats cross-attention and in-context conditioning | latent CIFAR-10 or MNIST through your `ConvVAE`, DiT-S-sized models with patch sizes 1–2 (MNIST latents force $p = 1$) | needs a good autoencoder first; budget the encoding pass |

other papers are welcome if your claims table states the claim and the scaled-down test before you start. avoid claims that need ImageNet-scale training, text conditioning with a large pretrained encoder, or more than a few hours per run on your hardware.

## compute budget

everything must run on a laptop: an Apple M-series machine (PyTorch's `mps` device; `mlfs.torchnn.get_device()` picks it) or, more slowly, a CPU.

- **total**: at most about **10 GPU-hours** on `mps` for all final runs, including seeds. diffusion models are compute-hungry: a CIFAR-10 model that produces recognizable objects needs far more than that, so choose claims about *comparisons* and *samplers*, and scale resolution, width and steps down until three seeds per configuration fit.
- **per run**: aim for 10–30 minutes of training. sampling is not free: a 1,000-step DDPM sample of 2,000 CIFAR images is 1,000 forward passes over 2,000 images, often longer than the FID computation and comparable to a short training run. budget it explicitly.
- **a small configuration** (under 2 minutes, CPU) that exercises every code path, used for debugging and for anyone checking your code.
- **reference throughputs measured in this course** (they vary with load; measure your own): chapter 21's capstone trained four families on binarized MNIST in about an hour on `mps`; chapter 22's capstone trained a 1.5M-parameter class-conditional `TimeUNet` on CIFAR-10 at about 0.43 s per step of 128 images (2,500 steps, 18 minutes per model, on a shared M2), and sampled 1,000 images with 25 guided network calls in about 30 s; topic 04's lab trains a 16-channel `TimeUNet` on MNIST at about 0.19 s per step of 128 digits (1,000 steps by default, 4,000 in its longer run) and samples it with up to eight samplers.
- record wall-clock time per run and in total, the hardware, and what you'd expect at the paper's scale.

## deliverables

1. **the claims table** (committed before the main runs), the list of gaps, the scale-down argument, and the metric calibration (feature network, number of samples, real-data floor).
2. **code**: a marimo notebook (or a small package) with the models, samplers, sanity checks and experiment runner; one command (or one notebook run) per figure; the small and full configurations; a saved log of every run, and the saved EMA weights of every final model.
3. **the reproducibility report** (3–6 pages, markdown or LaTeX):
   - *summary*: each claim, and for each: reproduced / partly / not, in one sentence with numbers and intervals;
   - *setup*: data, models, every hyperparameter, seeds, budgets, samplers and their NFE, the evaluation protocol, and every deviation from the paper;
   - *results*: the paper's figure or table next to yours; metric curves (against NFE, guidance or training steps) with bands over seeds; sample grids with fixed noise; comparisons as differences with intervals;
   - *discrepancies*: for every gap between your result and the paper's, your best explanation and at least one experiment that tests it (an unreported detail ablated, a longer run of one configuration, the paper's value of a hyperparameter you had guessed, a different feature network);
   - *reproducibility checklist*: seeds, library versions, hardware, runtimes, the metric's exact definition, and what someone would need to rerun everything (Pineau et al., 2021);
   - *what you'd do with 100× the compute*.
4. **a one-paragraph abstract** of the reproduction, the kind you'd post with the code.

## assessment

there are no checks. grade yourself (or have a peer grade you) on each criterion, 0–3; a solid milestone scores at least 12 out of 15, with no zeros.

| criterion | what a 3 looks like |
|---|---|
| **claims** | precise, testable claims and success criteria written before the runs, phrased as comparisons that survive the scale-down; the scale-down argued |
| **implementation** | samplers checked against closed forms on Gaussian data; a 2-D toy fully covered; runs reproducible from their seeds; the paper's details implemented or every deviation listed |
| **evidence** | ≥ 3 training seeds per configuration, two sample draws, the real-data floor of the metric, intervals everywhere; no claim stronger than its interval |
| **discrepancy analysis** | every mismatch with the paper investigated with at least one targeted experiment; evaluation differences (sampler, NFE, feature network, sample count) separated from modelling differences |
| **report** | a reader could rerun everything, would know which numbers are course-internal, what was reproduced, what wasn't, and how sure you are |
