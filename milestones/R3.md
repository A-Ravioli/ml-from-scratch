# R3 · research milestone: reproduce a reinforcement learning paper

*after chapter 24 · about 40–60 hours · L5 (open): a question, a protocol and a budget; no starter code, no checks*

## why this milestone

deep RL is the part of machine learning where published results are hardest to reproduce. results depend on seeds, on unreported implementation details, on evaluation protocols that differ from paper to paper, and on environments whose versions change; Henderson et al. (2018) showed that two correct implementations of the same algorithm, or the same implementation on two sets of five seeds, can produce learning curves that look like different algorithms. reproducing an RL result at small scale is therefore the best possible training in the habits chapter 24 kept insisting on: many seeds, intervals, greedy evaluation, ablations stated before they're run, and honesty about what didn't work.

you'll pick one paper, identify one or two of its central empirical claims, reproduce them at a scale that fits a laptop CPU, and write a reproducibility report. "the claim held", "it held only partly" and "it didn't hold, and here's my best explanation" are all successful outcomes; an unclear report is not.

## the protocol

this milestone follows the reproduction protocol of chapter 30 · topic 02 (read it first if you haven't; R1 and R2 used it too). in brief:

1. **read the paper three times** (ch 30 · topic 01): once for the claims, once for the method, once for every detail of the experiments (architecture, hyperparameters, number of seeds, evaluation protocol, how curves were smoothed, what the error bars are).
2. **write the claims table** *before* writing code: each claim you'll test, the paper's evidence (figure or table, number of seeds, uncertainty), the scaled-down version you'll run and why it tests the same claim (which quantity must be preserved; see R1's step 4), and what result would count as "reproduced", "partly reproduced" or "not reproduced". fix a margin per claim before running: *reproduced* means the 90% interval for the effect lies inside the band the claim predicts (the paper's value ± that margin, or above a stated minimum effect when only the direction can carry over to your scale); an interval that merely excludes 0 confirms the direction, not the size (chapter 30 · topic 02 defines the verdicts). commit it.
3. **list the gaps**: every detail the paper doesn't specify and the choice you made, with your reason. these are the usual suspects when a result doesn't reproduce.
4. **implement from the paper**, using your `mlfs` modules where they fit (`mlfs.deeprl`, `mlfs.rl`, `mlfs.torchnn`, `mlfs.stats`). reading the authors' code is allowed *after* your first full run, to diagnose discrepancies; note in the report what you changed because of it.
5. **sanity checks before the real runs**: the environment against its published equations (as chapter 24 did for CartPole, Pendulum and the acrobot), the losses on hand-computed examples, a tiny task with a known optimum where your agent must succeed over ≥ 5 seeds, and a run that reproduces bit for bit from its seed.
6. **run the experiment** with a fixed budget and at least 10 seeds per configuration (5 only if a single run exceeds your compute budget, stated as a limitation). log everything needed to redraw every figure.
7. **analyse**: learning curves with bootstrap bands over seeds, final greedy performance with intervals, and the paper's comparison (e.g. method A against method B) as a difference of means with a bootstrap interval and a permutation test (your capstone's `compare_seeds`), judged against the margin from step 2.
8. **report** (template below).

## choosing a paper

choose a paper with a clear, testable claim that survives being scaled down. the environments must be ones you implement yourself as NumPy `mlfs.rl.Env`s (Gymnasium is not installed in this course; if you have it, wrap it with a thin adapter and say so), validated against their published descriptions. good choices:

| paper | claim to reproduce | small-scale setting | notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mnih et al. (2015), DQN; with Young & Tian (2019), MinAtar | replay and the target network are both needed; DQN learns from pixels | one MinAtar game (Breakout or Space Invaders, 10 × 10 × channels), implemented in NumPy from the MinAtar paper's description | the ablation of replay and target networks is the classic result (Extended Data Table 3); compare with topic 01's CartPole finding |
| van Hasselt et al. (2016), double DQN | DQN overestimates; double DQN reduces it and scores higher | a task with many actions and noisy rewards (a gridworld with stochastic rewards, or a MinAtar game), Q estimates against Monte Carlo returns | reproduce the "estimate vs truth" figure; topic 01's lab found little bias on CartPole: explain the difference |
| Schulman et al. (2017), PPO | PPO-clip beats the adaptive-KL and no-clipping variants on continuous control | Pendulum and the acrobot (or a continuous acrobot), ≤ 300k steps | Table 1 of the paper compares surrogate objectives; your `ppo_clip_loss` plus a KL-penalty variant |
| Engstrom et al. (2020), "implementation matters" | the code-level optimizations, not the clip, explain much of PPO's advantage over TRPO | CartPole and the acrobot, PPO with and without the optimizations, TRPO via topic 04's natural-gradient step plus a line search | extends topic 04's lab with more seeds and a real TRPO |
| Haarnoja et al. (2018b), SAC with automatic temperature | auto-tuned α matches the best fixed α across tasks | Pendulum and a continuous task of your own with a different reward scale | topic 05's lab is the pilot: scale the rewards by 10 and 0.1 and see which settings survive |
| Kumar et al. (2020), CQL | CQL outperforms offline DQN/BC on mixed-quality datasets | datasets from CartPole or the acrobot collected by random, medium and expert policies and mixtures | the D4RL-style "medium-replay" dataset is the interesting one: BC can't stitch, CQL can |
| Ross et al. (2011), DAgger | DAgger's error grows linearly in the horizon, BC's quadratically | the tightrope of topic 07's checks or CartPole with a hand-written expert, varying the horizon | plot cost against horizon on log–log axes and fit the slopes |
| Silver et al. (2017/2018), AlphaZero | self-play with a learned evaluator beats MCTS with rollouts at equal simulations | Connect Four on a reduced board (topic 06), with Dirichlet noise and symmetry augmentation | measure strength over training against fixed MCTS opponents, with Wilson intervals |

other papers are welcome if you can state the claim and the scaled-down test in your claims table before starting. avoid papers whose central claim needs Atari-scale compute or proprietary simulators.

## compute budget

everything must run on a laptop CPU: at most about **10 CPU-hours** for all final runs, with a small configuration (a few minutes) that exercises every code path. record wall-clock time per run, and state what you would expect at the paper's scale, and why.

## deliverables

1. **the claims table** (committed before the main runs) and the list of gaps.
2. **code**: a marimo notebook (or a small package) with the environments, the agents, the sanity checks and the experiment runner; one command per figure; a small and a full configuration.
3. **the reproducibility report** (3–6 pages, markdown or LaTeX):
   - *summary*: the claims, and for each: reproduced / partly / not, in one sentence with numbers;
   - *setup*: environments (with the validation you did), algorithms, every hyperparameter, seeds, budgets, evaluation protocol, and every deviation from the paper;
   - *results*: the paper's figure or table next to yours; learning curves with bands; final greedy performance with intervals; the comparisons as differences with intervals and p-values;
   - *discrepancies*: for every gap between your result and the paper's, your best explanation and any experiment that tests it (an ablation of an unreported detail, a larger budget on one configuration);
   - *reproducibility checklist*: seeds, versions, hardware, runtime, what someone would need to rerun everything;
   - *what you'd do with 100× the compute*.
4. **a one-paragraph abstract** of the reproduction, the kind you'd post with the code.

## assessment

there are no checks. grade yourself (or have a peer grade you) on each criterion, 0–3; a solid milestone scores at least 12 out of 15, with no zeros.

| criterion | what a 3 looks like |
|---|---|
| **claims** | precise, testable claims and success criteria written before the runs; the scale-down argued (why the small version tests the same thing) |
| **implementation** | environments validated against their published descriptions; losses checked by hand; a tiny task solved over ≥ 5 seeds; runs reproducible from their seeds |
| **evidence** | ≥ 10 seeds per configuration, bands and intervals everywhere, comparisons as differences with uncertainty; no claim stronger than its interval |
| **discrepancy analysis** | every mismatch with the paper investigated with at least one targeted experiment; unreported details identified and tested |
| **report** | a reader could rerun everything and would know what was reproduced, what wasn't, and how sure you are |
