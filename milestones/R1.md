# R1 · research milestone: reproduce a deep learning paper

*after chapter 18 · about 40–60 hours · L5 (open): a question, a protocol and a budget; no starter code, no checks*

## why this milestone

by now you've built, from scratch, almost every component of a 2017-era deep learning system: autograd, initialization and normalization, optimizers and schedules, convolutional and residual networks, recurrent networks and attention, transformers. you've also seen, lab after lab, how fragile a result can be: a ratio of learning rates that decides whether a 24-layer transformer trains, a forget-gate bias that decides how far an LSTM remembers, a test block whose loss sits 0.3 bits above its validation block.

a reproduction is where these come together. you'll pick one paper, identify one or two of its central empirical claims, rebuild the experiment at a scale that fits a laptop, and write a reproducibility report. it's the first of three milestones (R2 after chapter 22 for a generative model, R3 after chapter 24 for reinforcement learning), and the warm-up for the thesis of chapter 30. "the claim held", "it held only partly" and "it didn't hold at this scale, and here's my best explanation" are all successful outcomes; an unclear report is not.

## the protocol

this milestone follows the reproduction protocol of chapter 30 · topic 02, summarized here so the brief stands on its own (R2 and R3 use it too).

1. **read the paper three times**: once for the claims (abstract, introduction, figures), once for the method, and once for every detail of the experiments: architecture, initialization, optimizer and schedule, batch size, data preprocessing and augmentation, number of runs, how the reported numbers and error bars were computed. write down where each detail is (section, table, appendix, footnote).
2. **write the claims table before writing code.** for each claim you'll test: the paper's statement, its evidence (figure or table, number of runs, any uncertainty), the scaled-down experiment you'll run, and what result would count as *reproduced*, *partly reproduced* or *not reproduced*, as a concrete criterion (e.g. "the 56-layer plain network's final training error exceeds the 20-layer plain network's in at least 4 of 5 seeds, with a bootstrap interval for the difference that excludes 0"). commit it.
3. **list the gaps**: every detail the paper doesn't specify, and the choice you made with a one-line reason. these are the usual suspects when a result doesn't reproduce.
4. **argue the scale-down.** explain why your smaller experiment tests the same claim: which quantity must be preserved (depth for a degradation claim, data size relative to model size for a data-efficiency claim, sequence length relative to training length for an extrapolation claim), and which can shrink (width, number of epochs, image size, vocabulary).
5. **implement from the paper**, using your `mlfs` modules (`mlfs.cnn`, `mlfs.seq`, `mlfs.transformer`, `mlfs.train`, `mlfs.torchnn`, `mlfs.stats`). you may read the authors' code only *after* your first complete run, to diagnose a discrepancy; record every change you made because of it.
6. **sanity checks before the real runs**: shapes and parameter counts against the paper's tables, a tiny subset the model must overfit (loss near 0 on 64 examples), a run that reproduces bit for bit from its seed, and the baseline numbers your chapters already measured where they apply (chapter 16's CIFAR recipe, chapter 17's bits per character).
7. **run the experiment** with a fixed budget and **at least 5 seeds per configuration** (3 only if a single run exceeds a fifth of your compute budget, stated as a limitation). log everything needed to redraw every figure: per-step losses, per-epoch metrics, wall-clock time, configuration, seed, library versions.
8. **analyse**: curves with bands over seeds, final metrics with bootstrap intervals (`mlfs.stats.bootstrap_ci`), and the paper's comparison (method A against method B) as a difference of means with an interval and a permutation test (`mlfs.stats.permutation_test`). no claim stronger than its interval.
9. **report**, following the template in *deliverables*.

## choosing a paper

choose a paper whose central claim survives being scaled down, on data you can get: CIFAR-10, MNIST, tiny Shakespeare and the SMS spam collection are cached by `mlfs.datasets`; anything else (Multi30k, WikiText-2, a subset of ImageNet) you download yourself, once, and document. good choices, roughly from most to least compute-friendly:

| paper | claim to reproduce | small-scale setting | notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| He, Zhang, Ren & Sun (2016), ResNet | **degradation**: deeper *plain* networks have higher *training* error than shallower ones; residual connections remove the effect (their figure 6, on CIFAR-10) | the paper's own CIFAR family: plain and residual nets with $6n + 2$ layers, $n \in \{3, 9\}$ (20 and 56 layers), widths 16-32-64, built from `mlfs.cnn.BasicBlock` with `residual=False`/`True` | the classic choice, and the paper's CIFAR section is close to laptop scale already. chapter 16 · topic 04's lab is your pilot: scale it to the paper's depths and seeds |
| Zhang, Bengio, Hardt, Recht & Vinyals (2017), "rethinking generalization" | networks fit **random labels** perfectly, and the time to fit grows with the fraction of corrupted labels (their figure 1) | a 10,000-image CIFAR-10 subset, a small ResNet or MLP, label corruption 0, 0.2, 0.5, 0.8, 1.0, plus shuffled pixels | cheap and striking; the interesting part is the training-time curve and the test error as corruption grows |
| Srivastava, Hinton, Krizhevsky, Sutskever & Salakhutdinov (2014), dropout | dropout reduces test error of MLPs on MNIST across architectures (their table 2) | 2–3 hidden layers of 1,024 units, with and without dropout, max-norm as in the paper | small, but the paper's exact numbers need many epochs; reproduce the *gap*, not the absolute errors |
| Ioffe & Szegedy (2015), batch normalization | BN reaches the same accuracy in far fewer steps and tolerates much higher learning rates | MNIST MLP (their figure 1) and a CIFAR-10 convnet, a learning-rate sweep with and without BN | chapter 15 · topic 02's lab found the learning-rate tolerance; this asks for the paper's protocol and seeds |
| Frankle & Carbin (2019), lottery tickets | iterative magnitude pruning with rewinding to the *original* initialization finds subnetworks (at 10–20% of the weights) that train to the full network's accuracy; random re-initialization doesn't | LeNet-300-100 on MNIST, 5–10 pruning rounds of 20% | many short runs; budget the rounds × seeds carefully |
| Xiong et al. (2020), pre-LN vs post-LN | post-LN transformers need warmup; pre-LN ones train without it and converge faster | character-level LM on tiny Shakespeare, 12–24 layers, with your `TransformerBlock`; warmup lengths and learning rates swept | topic 03's lab is the pilot (one seed, one learning rate): add seeds, depths and the warmup sweep |
| Press, Smith & Lewis (2022), ALiBi | train short, test long: ALiBi extrapolates to longer inputs, sinusoidal and rotary encodings don't (their figure 1) | your `PositionalLM` on tiny Shakespeare (or WikiText-2 if you download it), training lengths 64 and 128, evaluation to 8× longer | topic 02's lab is the pilot; the reproduction needs several seeds and a converged in-distribution loss |
| Vaswani et al. (2017), the transformer | on translation, the transformer beats recurrent seq2seq models with attention in quality and in training time (their table 2), and label smoothing and warmup matter (table 3) | Multi30k English→German (29,000 sentence pairs; Elliott et al., 2016), your `EncoderDecoder` (its `src_mask` keeps padded source tokens out of both the encoder and the cross-attention) against chapter 17's `Seq2Seq`, BLEU with greedy or beam decoding (`mlfs.seq.beam_search`), equal wall-clock budgets | the CURRICULUM's suggested transformer result; you'll implement BLEU yourself (or use `sacrebleu` and say so). keep models small (2–3 layers, width 256) |
| Kaplan et al. (2020), scaling laws | test loss falls as a power law in parameters when data isn't the bottleneck, with an exponent near 0.076 for their setting (their figure 1) | the capstone's GPTs on tiny Shakespeare, 5–6 sizes, enough steps per size to approach convergence | a stretch: at 1M characters, data runs out quickly. a good report says where the power law stops and why |
| Dosovitskiy et al. (2021), ViT | ViTs lose to ResNets with little data and catch up as data grows (their figure 3) | ViT vs ResNet on CIFAR-10 subsets of 1k–50k images, as in topic 07's lab, with longer training and 3+ seeds | the crossover needs far more data than CIFAR-10; a careful *partial* reproduction (the trend) is the realistic goal |

other papers are welcome if your claims table states the claim and the scaled-down test before you start. avoid papers whose central claim needs ImageNet-scale training, proprietary data, or more than a few hours per run on your hardware.

## compute budget

everything must run on a laptop: an Apple M-series machine (PyTorch's `mps` device; `mlfs.torchnn.get_device()` picks it) or, more slowly, a CPU.

- **total**: at most about **10 GPU-hours** on `mps` (or about 30 CPU-hours) for all final runs, including seeds. that's roughly 50–100 runs of 5–10 minutes each.
- **per run**: aim for 2–10 minutes. if one run of the paper's setting would take an hour, shrink width, image size or epochs first, depth or data size last (they're usually what the claim is about).
- **a small configuration** (under 2 minutes, CPU) that exercises every code path, used for debugging and for anyone checking your code.
- **reference throughputs measured in this course** (they vary with load; measure your own): chapter 16's capstone trained a full-width ResNet-18 on CIFAR-10 for 20 epochs (about 7,800 steps of 128 images) in about 35 minutes on `mps`; chapter 17's 3.3M-parameter character LSTM took 0.26 s per step of 64 × 128 characters (10 minutes for 2,500 steps); chapter 18's capstone trained three GPTs (0.35M, 1.8M and 7.5M parameters, 2,000 steps of 64 × 128 characters each) in 25 minutes on a shared laptop GPU, 16 of them for the largest.
- record wall-clock time per run and in total, the hardware, and what you'd expect at the paper's scale.

## deliverables

1. **the claims table** (committed before the main runs), the list of gaps, and the scale-down argument.
2. **code**: a marimo notebook (or a small package) with the models, data pipeline, sanity checks and experiment runner; one command (or one notebook run) per figure; the small and full configurations; a saved log of every run.
3. **the reproducibility report** (3–6 pages, markdown or LaTeX):
   - *summary*: each claim, and for each: reproduced / partly / not, in one sentence with numbers and intervals;
   - *setup*: data, models, every hyperparameter, seeds, budgets, evaluation protocol, and every deviation from the paper;
   - *results*: the paper's figure or table next to yours; curves with bands over seeds; final metrics with intervals; the comparisons as differences with intervals and p-values;
   - *discrepancies*: for every gap between your result and the paper's, your best explanation and at least one experiment that tests it (an ablation of an unreported detail, a longer run of one configuration, the authors' value of a hyperparameter you had guessed);
   - *reproducibility checklist*: seeds, library versions, hardware, runtimes, what someone would need to rerun everything (the ML reproducibility checklist of Pineau et al., 2021 is a good template);
   - *what you'd do with 100× the compute*.
4. **a one-paragraph abstract** of the reproduction, the kind you'd post with the code.

## assessment

there are no checks. grade yourself (or have a peer grade you) on each criterion, 0–3; a solid milestone scores at least 12 out of 15, with no zeros.

| criterion | what a 3 looks like |
|---|---|
| **claims** | precise, testable claims and success criteria written before the runs; the scale-down argued (what is preserved, what is shrunk, and why the small experiment tests the same thing) |
| **implementation** | parameter counts and shapes checked against the paper; a tiny subset overfit; runs reproducible from their seeds; the paper's details implemented or every deviation listed |
| **evidence** | ≥ 5 seeds per configuration, bands and intervals everywhere, comparisons as differences with uncertainty and a permutation test; no claim stronger than its interval |
| **discrepancy analysis** | every mismatch with the paper investigated with at least one targeted experiment; unreported details identified and tested |
| **report** | a reader could rerun everything, and would know what was reproduced, what wasn't, and how sure you are |
