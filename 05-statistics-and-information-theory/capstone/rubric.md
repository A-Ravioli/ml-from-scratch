# 05 · capstone rubric

score each criterion 0–3. a solid capstone scores at least 9 out of 12, with no zeros.

| criterion | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **correctness** | tests fail | most tests pass | all tests pass; every interval and p-value in the write-up comes from your toolkit with a fixed seed | all pass, plus your own extra checks (e.g. `metric_ci` agrees with the normal-theory interval for accuracy on a large test set; your paired permutation p-value matches an exact enumeration on a tiny example; McNemar and the permutation test agree on the California comparisons) |
| **results** | accuracies without uncertainty | accuracies with intervals | every model with a 95% interval, the `compare_models` table against the income rule with BH, and the majority baseline reported | plus paired intervals stated in accuracy points, a clear separation of "significant" from "important" (a significant 1-point difference vs a non-significant one), and the test-set size needed to resolve a 1-point difference |
| **analysis** | none | describes the tables | explains why paired intervals are narrower (with the correlation of per-example correctness), and why nearest centroid's gain isn't significant while 15-NN's over 1-NN is | also identifies what the intervals leave out (training-set variability, the random split, spatial dependence between neighbouring districts) and runs at least one stretch experiment that measures one of them |
| **clarity** | hard to follow | readable | tables and the interval plot are labeled, and the narrative answers the six prompts | a peer could reproduce every number from the write-up alone (split seed, resampling counts, method, config, runtime) |

## what graders look for

- **comparing overlapping intervals instead of the difference.** "the two intervals overlap, so the models are indistinguishable" is a common argument, and the wrong test: two separate 95% intervals can overlap while the interval for the difference excludes 0, and how much they overlap depends on how correlated the models' errors are, which separate intervals can't see. on California, nearest centroid's and the income rule's intervals overlap heavily; the right object is the paired interval for their difference, $[-0.001, +0.020]$. for 15-NN vs 1-NN the paired interval, $[+0.023, +0.041]$, is about 30% narrower than an unpaired one, because the two models' per-example correctness has correlation 0.49. the write-up should make the difference the object of inference.
- **significance is not importance.** with 6,130 examples, tiny differences can be significant, and with 300 even large ones may not be. a strong write-up reports effect sizes with intervals first, and p-values second.
- **the right unit of resampling.** the test set is a sample of districts, so districts are what get resampled, all models together. resampling each model's predictions separately throws away the pairing.
- **honest scope.** the intervals answer "how much would these numbers move with a different test set drawn the same way?" they say nothing about a different training set, a different random split, or a different *population* (a different state, a different decade). the best write-ups notice that location-only 15-NN most likely wins *because* the split is random over districts: every test district has training neighbours a few kilometres away with similar house prices, which a split by region would remove (the spatial-split stretch goal tests this).
- **multiple comparisons, and the ones nobody counts.** five comparisons against a baseline is a small family, and BH barely changes anything here. choosing $k$ for k-NN by looking at test accuracy, or reporting only the best of many runs, is an uncounted family of comparisons, and no correction applied afterwards can fix it.
