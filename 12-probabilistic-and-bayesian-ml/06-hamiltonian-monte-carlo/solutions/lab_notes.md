## experiment 1: HMC versus random-walk Metropolis, at equal cost

**what people usually predict:** that HMC's $L + 1$ gradients per sample eat up its advantage, so at equal cost the two are within a small factor, perhaps with Metropolis ahead in high dimensions where gradients are "expensive".

**what happens:** (shipped seeds, 40,000 evaluations each) on the 2-D Gaussian with $\rho = 0.99$, Metropolis (acceptance 0.60) gets a minimum ESS of 42 for $x$ and 77 for $x^2$; HMC (1,904 iterations of 20 steps, acceptance 0.96) gets 773 and 1,316: **about 18×** more for both. on the 100-D Gaussian, Metropolis at its optimal acceptance (0.23) gets minimum ESS 54 ($x$) and 149 ($x^2$) from 40,000 steps; HMC (5,714 iterations of 6 steps, trajectory length 1.5, acceptance 0.94) gets 3,808 and 4,017: **about 70×** more per evaluation for the means and **about 27×** for the variances. (with a longer trajectory, $T = 2.5$, the ESS of $x$ hits the estimator's ceiling of $n\log_{10}n$ because successive draws become anti-correlated, which looks like "240×" but is mostly the cap, while the ESS of $x^2$ drops: experiment 3 explains why. the shipped setting avoids that.)

**why:** a random walk with step size $s$ needs about $(D/s)^2$ steps to travel a distance $D$, because its direction is random each time. on the ridge, $s$ is limited by the narrow direction (sd 0.1) while the ridge is 1.4 long: hundreds of steps per independent draw. HMC's trajectory moves in a consistent direction, so it travels $L\varepsilon$ in $L$ steps (linearly, not like a square root), along the ridge, guided by the gradient. in $d$ dimensions the random walk's step must shrink like $d^{-1/2}$ and its cost per independent sample grows like $d$; HMC's step needs only to shrink like $d^{-1/4}$ (the energy error over $d$ coordinates grows like $d\varepsilon^4$), so its advantage grows with dimension. the price: HMC needs gradients, and it needs its step size and trajectory length tuned (a step size beyond the stability limit, experiment 2, is catastrophic).

## experiment 2: the stability cliff

**what people usually predict:** a smooth decline in acceptance as the step grows, like Metropolis.

**what happens:** in 1-D the acceptance stays above 0.9 up to $\varepsilon \approx 1.4$, falls to 0.61 at 1.7 and 0.26 at 1.95, and then drops off a cliff: **0.06 at $\varepsilon = 2.0$ and exactly 0 from 2.05 on**. the energy error of one trajectory (right panel) is below 1 for every $\varepsilon < 2$ and then explodes: $10^3$ at 2.05, $10^{12}$ at 2.6. in 100-D the acceptance is lower everywhere (0.83 at 0.5, 0.54 at 0.95) and is essentially zero well before the cliff, from about 1.5 on (apart from one resonance spike, 0.35 at $\varepsilon = 1.9$). the curves are jagged. for a fixed $L = 10$, one leapfrog step on a Gaussian is a rotation by an angle $\theta(\varepsilon)$ with $\cos\theta = 1 - \varepsilon^2/2$ (in slightly distorted coordinates), and the energy error of a trajectory returns to (almost) zero whenever $L\theta(\varepsilon)$ is a multiple of $\pi$, every half turn of the leapfrog map. near $\varepsilon = 2$, $\theta \to \pi$ quickly, so these zeros come thick and fast: that produces resonance spikes such as 0.94 at $\varepsilon = 1.9$ in 1-D. (jittering $\varepsilon$ or $L$ from iteration to iteration smooths them out.)

**why:** one leapfrog step on $U = q^2/2$ is the linear map from the worked example, with trace $2 - \varepsilon^2$ and determinant 1. for $\varepsilon < 2$ its eigenvalues are $e^{\pm i\theta}$: the map is a slightly distorted rotation and the energy error stays bounded, whatever the trajectory length. at $\varepsilon = 2$ the eigenvalues meet at $-1$ and become real for $\varepsilon > 2$, one of them larger than 1 in magnitude: the orbit is a hyperbola, the energy grows geometrically with every step, and nothing is accepted. that's a **divergent transition** in Stan's language: a trajectory that went through a region whose curvature (here $1/\sigma^2 = 1$) was too high for the step size. in 100-D the energy error is a sum of 100 per-coordinate errors, so its spread grows like $\sqrt d$ times the 1-D one, and the acceptance falls long before the cliff; keeping it fixed requires $\varepsilon \propto d^{-1/4}$. this is why Stan tunes $\varepsilon$ to a target acceptance (0.8 by default) during warm-up, and why a posterior with a region of very high curvature (the capstone's funnel) can't be sampled with any single step size.

## experiment 3: how long should a trajectory be?

**what people usually predict:** "longer is better" (more distance covered), or that there's a sweet spot after which more steps just waste compute.

**what happens:** (600 iterations per point, step 0.1) the ESS per iteration is 0.03 at $T = 0.3$ (a near random walk), climbs to about 0.9 for both $x$ and $x^2$ around $T = \pi/2$, and then the two curves split. around $T = \pi$ the ESS of $x$ reads **2.78 per iteration**, which is exactly $\log_{10}600$, the ceiling set by topic 05's floor on $\tau$ (the chain is anti-correlated, and its true ESS is larger still, so read it as "at least 2.78") while the ESS of $x^2$ crashes to **0.01**. near $T = 4.7$ both are good again (1.0 and 0.72), and at $T = 2\pi$ both crash to **0.01**: the sampler barely moves.

**why:** for a standard Gaussian the exact dynamics are a rotation of the $(q, p)$ plane with period $2\pi$. starting from $(q_0, p_0)$ with $p_0 \sim \mathcal N(0, 1)$, after time $T$ the position is $q_T = q_0\cos T + p_0\sin T$. at $T = \pi/2$, $q_T = p_0$: a fresh independent draw (ESS ≈ 1). at $T = \pi$, $q_T = -q_0$: the chain flips sign deterministically, which is wonderful for the mean (the errors cancel, ESS ≫ $n$) and useless for anything even like $x^2$ (it never changes). at $T = 2\pi$, $q_T = q_0$: no movement at all. leapfrog with a small step follows this closely, which is why the resonances are sharp. a real posterior has many scales, each with its own period, so any fixed $T$ resonates badly in some direction. the remedies: draw $L$ at random each iteration (jittering), or let the trajectory decide when to stop, which is NUTS: integrate until the U-turn criterion fires, which happens when the trajectory reaches the far point of its swing, as your `u_turn_steps` shows: starting at rest at $q = 1$ on a unit Gaussian that's half a period (about 31 steps of 0.1, time $\pi$); starting at the mode with $p = 1$ on a Gaussian of sd 5 it's a quarter period (about 79 steps, time $5\pi/2$). (the two numbers differ by starting point as well as by scale: from the same starting condition, the step count scales in proportion to the sd.) either way the trajectory length adapts to the local scale, and it stops before the resonance at a full period.
