# markov chain monte carlo · lab notes

## experiment 1: step size, acceptance, mixing

**what people usually predict:** that a high acceptance rate is a sign of a good sampler ("rejections are wasted work"), so the best scale is a small one with acceptance near 1.

**what happens:** (shipped seeds; ESS per step averaged over coordinates) in 1-D the best of the 25 scales is **2.11**, with acceptance **0.49** and 0.26 effective samples per step. in 20-D the best is **0.61**, acceptance **0.19**, and only 0.016 effective samples per step. at the smallest scale (0.05) the acceptance is 0.98 in 1-D and 0.92 in 20-D, and the ESS per step is about 0.001: a thousand steps per independent draw. at large scales the acceptance collapses to zero and the chain stops moving (in 20-D nothing at all is accepted beyond a scale of about 1, and a frozen chain is worth less than one sample). the ESS curve against acceptance (right panel) peaks near the classical values 0.44 (1-D) and 0.234 (high-dimensional), with a broad, flat top.

**why:** with tiny steps every proposal is accepted, but the chain is a random walk with small increments: to cross a distribution of width 1 with steps of size $s$ it needs about $1/s^2$ steps, so the ESS per step falls like $s^2$. with huge steps almost every proposal lands where the density is negligible and is rejected, so the chain repeats itself. the optimum balances step length against acceptance. in $d$ dimensions a proposal perturbs every coordinate, and the log-density change grows like $s^2d$, so the scale has to shrink like $1/\sqrt d$ (theory: $2.38/\sqrt{20} = 0.53$; measured best 0.61, within the flat top) and the optimal acceptance falls towards 0.234 (Roberts, Gelman & Gilks, 1997). the price is the ESS per step, which falls like $1/d$: here $0.26 \to 0.016$ going from 1 to 20 dimensions; at $d = 1000$ you'd need thousands of steps per independent draw. this $O(d)$ cost is what Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (topic 06) breaks.

## experiment 2: a chain that looks fine

**what people usually predict:** that a chain with an ESS of several hundred gives a trustworthy mean, and that something must look visibly wrong if it doesn't.

**what happens:** (shipped seeds) each chain stays in the mode it started near: chains 1 and 2 have means $-4.02$ and $-3.96$, chains 3 and 4 have $+4.04$ and $+4.00$, and every chain spends 0% or 100% of its time in the right-hand mode. each chain's ESS is healthy (235–370), its trace looks like stationary noise, and its 95% interval for the mean would be about $\pm0.1$, around the wrong answer (the truth is 0). $\hat R$ across the four chains is **4.41**. the histogram of all four chains together matches the target well, but only because we happened to start two chains in each mode.

**why:** ESS and the trace plot measure how well a chain explores *the region it's in*. a random walk with scale 0.7 would have to cross a valley 8 standard deviations wide where the density is $e^{-8} \approx 3\times10^{-4}$ times smaller, which takes far longer than 4,000 steps, so each chain is a perfectly good sampler of one mode and gives no hint that the other exists. $\hat R$ compares chains with each other: their means disagree by 8 while the within-chain sd is 1, so $\hat R \gg 1$. it detects the problem because the chains were started in different places. a chain started at 0 would roll into one mode and stay there, so it wouldn't help; to get the 50/50 split right you need a sampler that can jump (tempering, a mixture proposal) or a smarter model parameterization. and if all chains had started on the same side, $\hat R$ would have been 1: diagnostics detect disagreement, not missing mass.

## experiment 3: Gibbs in a narrow valley

**what people usually predict:** that Gibbs, which never rejects, mixes well at any correlation, or that it slows down modestly (a factor of 2–5) at $\rho = 0.99$.

**what happens:** (ESS of $x_1$ averaged over 4 seeds) the ESS per sweep is 0.92 at $\rho = 0$, 0.56 at 0.5, 0.099 at 0.9, 0.046 at 0.95, **0.0098 at 0.99** (102 sweeps per independent draw) and 0.0059 at 0.995 (169 sweeps). the measurements follow the dotted curve $(1 - \rho^2)/(1 + \rho^2)$ closely; at $\rho = 0$ and 0.5 the measured values sit a little below it because the ESS estimator is noisy and slightly biased down for nearly independent draws. the left panel shows the zig-zag: tiny axis-parallel steps creeping along the diagonal ellipse.

**why:** the conditional $x_1 \mid x_2$ has standard deviation $\sqrt{1 - \rho^2}$, which is 0.14 at $\rho = 0.99$: each update can only move $x_1$ that far, while the distribution stretches about $\sqrt{1 + \rho}\approx1.4$ along the diagonal. composing the two half-sweeps gives the AR(1) recursion $x_1^{(t+1)} = \rho^2x_1^{(t)} + \text{noise}$, so $\phi = \rho^2$ and ESS per sweep is $(1 - \rho^2)/(1 + \rho^2)$. the cure is a change of variables: in the rotated coordinates $u = (x_1 + x_2)/\sqrt2$, $v = (x_1 - x_2)/\sqrt2$ the two are independent and Gibbs mixes perfectly. in real models, correlated parameters (a slope and an intercept with uncentred data, a group mean and its group effects) produce exactly this pathology; reparameterizing the model, centering the predictors, or updating correlated blocks jointly is the standard remedy, and the non-centered parameterization in the capstone is the same idea applied to a funnel.
