# hidden markov models · lab notes

## experiment 1: decoding the casino

**what people usually predict:** that the six rule does reasonably (sixes are 5× likelier on the loaded die) and that Viterbi is a little better, or that Viterbi is nearly perfect.

**what happens:** on the shipped seed (300 rolls, loaded 35% of the time), Viterbi labels **83.0%** of the rolls correctly and posterior decoding 84.0%; "six means loaded" gets 71.3%, barely above the 65% you'd get by always saying "fair". Viterbi and posterior decoding disagree on only 5 rolls. the figure shows why: the posterior rises gradually through clusters of sixes and is near 0.5 at the edges of loaded stretches and in short stretches (a loaded spell of 3–5 rolls is often missed entirely: one or two sixes look like luck).

**why:** one roll carries little information: a six gives a likelihood ratio of 3 for "loaded", any other face a ratio of 0.6 for it. the six rule acts on that ratio alone, and since most loaded rolls *aren't* sixes, it misses most of the loaded stretch. the HMM pools the evidence of the neighbouring rolls through the transition matrix: a run of 15 rolls with five sixes multiplies those small ratios together into strong evidence, and the stickiness of the croupier (switches are rare) says the whole run probably shares one die. where the posterior hovers near 0.5 the uncertainty is genuine, not a failure: the data really can't tell. Viterbi finds the single best path, which is smooth by construction (it pays $\log 0.05$ for every switch); posterior decoding decides each roll separately and can flicker, and with zero-probability transitions it could even produce an impossible path. here they almost always agree.

## experiment 2: learning the casino from scratch

**what people usually predict:** that the learned parameters with the highest likelihood are close to the truth, and that their likelihood is at most that of the true parameters ("the truth explains the data best").

**what happens:** with 300 rolls (shipped seed), all 8 random starts end with a *higher* training log-likelihood than the true parameters ($-528.55$): between $-524.12$ and $-517.89$. none of them is close to the truth. the best two (seeds 2 and 3, $-518$) found a model where the "loaded" die shows a six only 30% of the time and the dice switch on about 80% of rolls; seeds 0 and 4 found a "fair" die that never shows a six; seeds 1 and 5 found the most casino-like answer ($P(6 \mid \text{loaded}) = 0.456$, switches 0.022 and 0.123) with a lower likelihood than the strange ones. different seeds stop at different local maxima, and several pairs of seeds land on the same one. at 5,000 rolls all 8 seeds agree: $P(6 \mid \text{loaded}) = 0.457$, $P(6 \mid \text{fair}) = 0.175$, switches 0.038 and 0.074, with log-likelihood $-8739.26$ against the truth's $-8743.94$.

**why:** maximum likelihood maximizes the probability of *this* sample, and with 300 rolls there are many patterns of noise (a run of fives, a stretch with no sixes) that a flexible 2-state model can explain better than the true model does. the true parameters are the best model *in expectation over datasets*; the MLE is the best model for the one you have, so its training likelihood is always at least the truth's, by about half the number of free parameters (here 13) in nats on average. the gap of 4.7 nats at 5,000 rolls is in that range; at 300 rolls the gaps of up to 10 nats show the extra freedom being used to fit noise. the likelihood surface of an HMM is non-convex with many local maxima (and every maximum appears twice, once per labelling of the states), so EM's answer depends on where it starts; with little data even the global maximum is far from the truth. the practical lessons: run EM from several starts, prefer the best likelihood but check it on held-out data, and add priors (pseudo-counts) when data are scarce.

## experiment 3: when memory stops helping

**what people usually predict:** that Viterbi keeps a solid edge over the six rule however often the croupier switches, or that it drops to the "always fair" 66.7%.

**what happens:** with loaded stretches of 40 rolls on average Viterbi gets 90.9% and posterior decoding 93.3%; with the casino's 10 rolls, 79.3% and 80.2%; with 5, 73.9% and 76.0%. by stretches of 2.5 rolls Viterbi (69.9%) is *worse* than the six rule (72.5%), and from about 1.7 rolls on every method sits at the six rule's 72% (the six rule stays flat at 72–73% throughout, as computed: 72.2%).

**why:** the HMM's advantage is pooling evidence from neighbouring rolls, and that only helps if neighbours share the die. as the stretches shorten, the neighbours carry less and less information about the current roll; when the switch probabilities sum to 1 (loaded stretches of 1.5 rolls) the die is redrawn independently each roll, the hidden states are i.i.d., and the best you can do is decide each roll on its own evidence, which is exactly the six rule. (beyond that point the states start to *alternate*, so the neighbours carry a little information again: the last two points creep up to 73%.) posterior decoding converges to it from above, because it maximizes the expected number of correct labels, which is the accuracy. Viterbi maximizes something else, the probability of the whole path being right, and for stretches of a few rolls that objective prefers smooth, mostly-fair paths that skip short loaded spells: it gives up per-roll accuracy, and falls below the memoryless rule. choose the decoder that matches the loss you care about.
