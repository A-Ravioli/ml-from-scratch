## experiment 1: where is the model unsure?

**what people usually predict:** that both models are most uncertain far from the data, at $x = 3$, and that the Bayesian machinery "knows" when it's extrapolating.

**what happens:** with the shipped seed, the cubic polynomial's predictive standard deviation is 0.176 inside the data, 0.181 in the gap and **3.81** at $x = 3$: the error bars fan out dramatically. the Gaussian-bump model does the opposite of what intuition says: 0.179 inside the data, **0.358** in the gap at $x = 0$, and only **0.289** at $x = 3$. it's more confident far outside the data than in the middle of it, and beyond $|x| \approx 1.7$ its band is a flat strip around a constant mean that ignores the true sine wave completely. (the evidence-optimized noise sd is 0.171 for both.)

**why:** the weight-uncertainty term is $\phi(x)^\top S_N\phi(x)$. polynomial features grow like $x^3$, so this term grows like $x^6$ and the variance explodes. the bumps all vanish far from their centers, so $\phi(x) \to (1, 0, \dots, 0)$ and the term collapses to the posterior variance of the constant weight, which 40 points pin down well. in the gap, by contrast, the two bumps nearest $x = 0$ are only weakly constrained by data, so their weights are uncertain, and that shows. the model isn't "detecting" extrapolation at all: its predictive distribution is uncertainty *within its own hypothesis space*, and the bump model's hypothesis space contains only functions that are flat far from $[-1, 1]$. to get uncertainty that returns to the prior level away from the data you need basis functions everywhere, which in the limit of infinitely many bumps is a Gaussian process with a squared-exponential kernel (ch 09; Rasmussen & Williams, 2006, §2.1). the polynomial's wide bands aren't more "honest" either: they come from a global basis whose extrapolations are wild, and the true function is outside them by $x = 1.5$.

## experiment 2: the evidence picks a degree

**what people usually predict:** that the evidence, like the training error, prefers the most flexible model, or that it's a noisy criterion that needs a held-out set to be useful.

**what happens:** with 30 points (shipped seed) the log evidence by degree is $-9.5, -9.6, -9.4, -2.0, -2.5, -3.1, -3.8, -4.4, -5.0, -5.4$: a jump of 7.4 nats at degree 3, then a steady decline. the highest evidence is at **degree 3**, the true one, which also has the lowest test RMSE (0.204, against a noise sd of 0.2). the training RMSE is lowest at degree 4 (it isn't strictly monotone here, because each degree's $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are re-optimized, so higher degrees are also more strongly regularized). with 200 points the picture is the same, sharper (evidence peaks at degree 3 by 2.2 nats; the test errors of degrees 3–9 are equal within 0.001). with 10 points the evidence is flat ($\approx -0.1$ for every degree): with so few, noisy points, "the weights are all zero and everything is noise" explains the data as well as any cubic, and evidence maximization drives $\alpha$ to its upper clip ($10^{12}$). a grid search over $(\alpha, \beta)$ confirms this is the true maximum, not a bug.

**why:** the evidence is a normalized distribution over datasets. a degree-9 model with a broad prior can generate wiggly datasets of all kinds, so it spends its probability on many datasets that weren't observed; a degree-2 model can't produce the cubic's shape at all. the evidence peaks at the simplest model that's adequate. in the $d \times d$ formula you can watch it happen: the fit term $-E$ improves only a little past degree 3, while $\frac d2\log\alpha - \frac12\log|A|$ (the log of prior volume over posterior volume) charges for every extra direction the data pin down. by the chain rule the evidence is also the product of one-step-ahead predictive probabilities, which is why it tracks the test error: it's a form of sequential out-of-sample validation that uses all the data. its weaknesses: it depends on the prior (a much broader prior on the weights lowers the evidence of every big model, cross-validation doesn't care), and it measures fit *within* the model, so with very little data it can happily prefer "nothing but noise".

## experiment 3: one point at a time

**what people usually predict:** that each data point shrinks the posterior blob a bit, uniformly, like a circle getting smaller.

**what happens:** after one point the posterior is a long, thin ridge (shipped seed: standard deviations 0.150 and 0.707 along its principal axes, an elongation of 4.7); the wide direction is exactly as wide as the prior (0.707 $= 1/\sqrt{\alpha}$). all six sampled lines pass close to the observed point and pivot around it with every slope. the second point, at $x \approx -0.52$ (the first was at $-0.83$), narrows the wide direction only slightly (0.600); after 20 points spread over $[-1, 1]$ the posterior is a small ellipse (0.044 by 0.089) around the true weights, and the sampled lines nearly coincide.

**why:** one point says $w_0 + w_1x_1 \approx y_1$, which is a line in weight space: a whole family of (intercept, slope) pairs explains it equally well. the posterior precision gains the rank-one term $\beta\phi_1\phi_1^\top$, which adds precision only along $\phi_1 = (1, x_1)$ and none in the perpendicular direction, so the posterior is narrow across the line and as wide as the prior along it. that perpendicular direction corresponds to "rotating the line about the data point", which is why the sampled lines pivot. a second point at nearly the same $x$ adds precision along almost the same direction, so it barely helps; a point far away (large $|x_2 - x_1|$) has $\phi_2$ pointing in a different direction and would collapse the ridge to a small blob. this is the intuition behind optimal experimental design and active learning: choose the next input where the posterior is widest in the direction $\phi(x)$ probes, i.e. where the predictive variance is largest.
