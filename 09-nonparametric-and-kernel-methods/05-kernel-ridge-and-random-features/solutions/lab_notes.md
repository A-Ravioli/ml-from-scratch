## experiment 1: approximating a real Gram matrix

**what people usually predict:** that two approximations with the same number of features are about equally good, or that random features win because they're "unbiased".

**what happens:** at $D = 100$ the relative Frobenius error is **0.307** for random Fourier features and **0.0405** for Nyström, **8× smaller**; the best possible rank-100 approximation (from the eigenvalues) is 0.0103. at $D = 1{,}000$: **0.093** against **0.0045**, 20× smaller. the random-feature curve is a straight line of slope $-1/2$ on the log-log plot; the Nyström curve bends down too, but nowhere near as fast as the optimum: it's about 4× above the best rank-$D$ error at $D = 100$, about 100× at $D = 500$ and about 2,000× at $D = 1{,}000$, where the eigenvalues beyond the $D$-th are tiny and the best rank-$D$ error collapses. the eigenvalues of $K$ fall by a factor of 100 within the first 25 and by almost $10^4$ by the 300th.

**why:** a random-feature Gram entry is an average of $D$ independent terms with variance of order 1, so its error is $O(1/\sqrt D)$ whatever the data look like: it spends features on frequencies the data never excite. Nyström projects onto the span of $m$ actual data points (on paper 3), so its error is governed by how much of $K$ lies outside the top-$m$ eigendirections, and for a smooth kernel on real data that's very little: the spectrum decays fast because the districts are concentrated in a few clusters (cities) and the RBF kernel at this width can't distinguish nearby points. random landmarks don't pick the optimal directions (the leading eigenvectors), so Nyström's error decays far more slowly than the optimum once the spectrum has dropped off; smarter landmark choices (k-means centres, leverage scores) close part of the gap. offline, on 1,000 synthetic Gaussian points, the gap at $D = 100$ is only 3× (0.65 against 0.21), because isotropic Gaussian data in 8-D has a flatter spectrum.

## experiment 2: does a better Gram matrix mean a better model?

**what people usually predict:** that the model's test error tracks the Gram error, so Nyström's 8–20× better approximation means a much better model, and that you need $D$ close to $n$ to match exact KRR.

**what happens:** exact kernel ridge on 4,000 districts has test RMSE **0.555** (predicting the mean: 1.183). Nyström gets 0.886, 0.794, **0.638**, 0.576, **0.557**, 0.554 at $D = 10, 30, 100, 300, 1000, 2000$; random features 0.986, 0.856, **0.636**, 0.596, **0.567**, 0.565. at $D = 100$ the two are tied (0.638 vs 0.636) despite the 8× difference in Gram error; Nyström is within 1% of exact KRR at $D = 1{,}000$ (a quarter of $n$), random features are still 2% behind at $D = 2{,}000$. exact KRR took 2.5 s to fit; the feature methods took 1–40 ms up to $D = 300$, and 0.2–3 s at $D = 1{,}000$–$2{,}000$ (Nyström's $W^{-1/2}$ eigendecomposition dominates there).

**why:** the test error has two parts: the statistical error of learning from 4,000 noisy points, which exact KRR also pays, and the extra error from the approximate kernel. once the approximation error is small compared with the statistical error, improving it further buys nothing, which is why both curves flatten near 0.555 and why a 10× better Gram matrix doesn't give a 10× better model. at small $D$, both methods are limited by the number of directions they can represent rather than by noise in the kernel. the costs scale differently: exact KRR is $O(n^3)$, so at $n = 40{,}000$ it would take about 1,000 times longer (and 13 GB for $K$), while ridge on $D$ features is $O(nD^2 + D^3)$, only 10 times longer for the same $D$. that's the whole point of the approximations: at large $n$, a few hundred to a few thousand features buy nearly all of KRR's accuracy.

## experiment 3: from interpolation to over-smoothing

**what people usually predict:** that the best model uses roughly one degree of freedom per data point ("it's nonparametric"), or that df at tiny $\alpha$ is exactly 40.

**what happens:** at $\alpha = 10^{-6}$ the effective degrees of freedom are **18.1** and the fit swings through every noisy point with RMSE **1.741** against the true $\sin 2x$; at $\alpha = 100$, df is near 0.3 and the curve is flattened towards 0. the best $\alpha$ on the grid is **0.32**, with **df = 7.6** and RMSE **0.128**; the slider's default $\alpha = 0.1$ gives df 9.1 and RMSE 0.138.

**why:** $\mathrm{df}(\alpha) = \sum_k \lambda_k/(\lambda_k + \alpha)$ counts the eigendirections of $K$ that survive the shrinkage. with $\gamma = 5$, the kernel's length scale is about 0.3, so 40 points on $[0, 3]$ are heavily correlated and most eigenvalues of $K$ are tiny: 22 of the 40 are below $10^{-6}$, which is why df is 18 rather than 40 even at $\alpha = 10^{-6}$ (and why such a small $\alpha$ is numerically treacherous: the system is nearly singular). the true function, $\sin 2x$ on $[0, 3]$, is smooth and needs only a handful of directions; every extra direction mostly fits noise. the right $\alpha$ balances the noise variance against the signal in each direction: directions whose eigenvalue $\lambda_k$ is small compared with $\alpha$ are dominated by noise and get shrunk away. topic 06 makes that precise: in a Gaussian process, $\alpha$ *is* the noise variance (here $0.3^2 = 0.09$, close to the best $\alpha$), and the marginal likelihood chooses it from the data.
