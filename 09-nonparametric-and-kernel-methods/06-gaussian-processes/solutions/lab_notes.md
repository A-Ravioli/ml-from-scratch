## experiment 1: what do the kernels believe?

**what people usually predict:** that the three kernels give similar curves, since they share a length scale and a variance; and that the posterior band between observations 3 units apart is roughly as narrow as at the observations.

**what happens:** the RBF prior samples are smooth, slowly bending curves; Matérn-5/2 samples are smooth too, but bend more sharply and wiggle more on small scales; Matérn-3/2 samples look rough, with visible jitter at small scales. after six observations, every posterior pinches to (almost) zero width at the data, and opens up between and beyond them. midway between the observations at 3 and 6, the posterior standard deviation is **0.822** for the RBF, **0.903** for Matérn-5/2 and **0.921** for Matérn-3/2, against a prior standard deviation of 1: 1.5 length scales away from the nearest data, the GP has learned little. to the right of the last observation and left of the first, the band widens back to the prior.

**why:** the kernels encode different smoothness, and you can read it off their expansion near $r = 0$. how many times a GP's samples are (mean-square) differentiable is set by the first *odd* power of $|r|$ in the kernel: a first odd term $|r|^{2k+1}$ means $k$ derivatives. the RBF kernel $e^{-r^2/2} = 1 - \frac{r^2}{2} + \frac{r^4}{8} - \dots$ is analytic, with only even powers, so its samples are infinitely differentiable. Matérn-3/2 expands as $1 - \frac32 r^2 + \sqrt3\,|r|^3 - \dots$: the first odd term is $|r|^3$, so its samples are once differentiable. Matérn-5/2 has $1 - \frac56 r^2 + \frac{25}{24} r^4 - \dots$ with its first odd term at $|r|^5$, so its samples are twice differentiable. the odd terms make the correlation fall off faster at small distances, so nearby values of a Matérn function decorrelate faster than under the RBF kernel with the same length scale, most of all for Matérn-3/2. that faster decorrelation is also why the rougher kernels give wider bands between the data: they let the function do more in between. at the observations the noise variance ($10^{-4}$) is tiny, so the posterior variance there is about $10^{-4}$. move the slider to a large $\ell$ and the three posteriors nearly agree (all smooth, all confident); at small $\ell$ they all revert to the prior between the points, the Matérn kernels fastest.

## experiment 2: two explanations of the same data

**what people usually predict:** that a good optimizer finds the obvious sine wave from any start, and that the two answers differ by a small amount.

**what happens:** started from $\ell = 5$, noise 0.5, L-BFGS converges to "everything is noise": signal variance $6.6 \times 10^{-8}$, noise variance 1, and a meaningless length scale of 1,330. the mean is flat at 0 and the band covers the data. LML **−35.47**. started from $\ell = 0.3$, it finds the sine: $\ell = 0.45$, signal variance 1.1, noise variance 0.088. LML **−25.88**, **9.6 nats** better, a likelihood ratio of about 15,000. with 6 restarts from the long start, the fit reaches **−25.88** too. the landscape shows a peak at short length scales and low noise share, a cliff for $\ell \gtrsim 1$ with little noise (a smooth function can't pass near 25 points that oscillate every 2 units), and a wide flat plateau at noise share near 1, where the length scale doesn't matter.

**why:** the data are consistent with two stories, and the evidence scores both. "a smooth function plus a lot of noise" explains the data as a spread around 0 with variance 1: a poor fit, but a very simple model. "a sine wave with period about 2 plus a little noise" fits much better at the price of more flexibility, and on 25 points the fit wins decisively. a gradient method only climbs: from $\ell = 5$, shortening the length scale first makes things *worse* (the model must explain the oscillations with a function that can't follow them, so it runs into the cliff), so the gradient points to a longer length scale and less signal, and the optimizer slides onto the plateau, where the gradient in $\ell$ vanishes because the signal variance is zero. restarts sample starting points across the basins; each costs another optimization run (a few dozen Cholesky factorizations here), and nothing guarantees enough of them for higher-dimensional hyperparameter spaces.

## experiment 3: honest error bars on real data

**what people usually predict:** "95% intervals" from any GP are roughly right, or roughly right only if the hyperparameters are perfect.

**what happens:** the learned Matérn-5/2 GP ($\ell = 7.26$ in standardized units, signal variance 10.56, noise variance 0.415) has test RMSE **0.647** (in \$100,000) and its 95% predictive intervals cover **95.2%** of 2,000 test districts, with a mean width of 2.71. the hand-picked GP ($\ell = 1$, $\sigma_f^2 = 1$, $\sigma_n^2 = 0.01$) has RMSE **0.759** and covers only **80.1%**, with narrower intervals (1.91). the histogram of standardized residuals is more peaked than $\mathcal N(0, 1)$ with heavier tails; grouped by posterior standard deviation, the actual error is *below* the predicted one for the most certain fifth of districts (0.58 vs 0.65) and for the least certain fifth (0.69 vs 0.85), and about right in between. the RBF GP's mean and your `KernelRidge` with $\gamma = 1/(2\ell^2)$, $\alpha = \sigma_n^2/\sigma_f^2$ agree to **$1.2 \times 10^{-8}$**.

**why:** with hand-picked hyperparameters the GP believes the noise variance is 0.01, i.e. a typical error of \$10,000, when the actual residual spread is about \$65,000 (the RMSE); its intervals are far too confident, and it fits the training data too closely (short length scale, little noise), so it predicts worse too. maximizing the evidence learns a noise variance of 0.415, which matches the squared residual scale, so the intervals are calibrated *on average*. "on average" hides structure: the GP assumes Gaussian noise with one variance everywhere, while house prices have heteroscedastic, heavy-tailed errors (cheap rural districts are easy to predict, the expensive coast is not, and the target is capped at \$500,000), so the standardized residuals are too peaked in the middle and too heavy in the tails, and the GP's own uncertainty $\sigma_*$ (which only measures distance from the training data) doesn't track the actual difficulty. the agreement with kernel ridge is on paper 1: the posterior mean *is* the KRR predictor with $\alpha = \sigma_n^2/\sigma_f^2$ (dividing the system by $\sigma_f^2$ turns $\sigma_f^2 K_1 + \sigma_n^2 I$ into $K_1 + \alpha I$); the remaining $10^{-8}$ is the jitter.
