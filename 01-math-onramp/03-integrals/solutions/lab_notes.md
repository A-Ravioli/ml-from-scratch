## experiment 1: convergence rates

**what people usually predict:** that the rules get better in the order left → midpoint → trapezoid → Simpson, often with the trapezoid rule ahead of the midpoint rule ("trapezoids hug the curve, rectangles don't"). most people expect Simpson to keep improving as long as you add strips.

**what happens:** for $e^x$ on $[0, 1]$ the measured orders are **1.0** (left), **2.0** (midpoint), **2.0** (trapezoid) and **4.0** (Simpson): on the log-log plot, four straight lines with slopes $-1, -2, -2, -4$. the midpoint and trapezoid lines are parallel, and the midpoint line sits *below* the trapezoid line by a factor of 2. Simpson's error falls from $6 \times 10^{-4}$ at $n = 2$ to about $4 \times 10^{-11}$ at $n = 128$, then hits a floor around $10^{-15}$–$10^{-16}$ and wanders up and down there. (if you picked $\frac1{1 + x^2}$: left, midpoint and trapezoid behave the same way, but Simpson shows an order near 6. see the "why" below.)

**why:** each order is the power of $h$ in the error formula: one triangle-shaped sliver of size $\sim f'h^2$ per strip for the left rule (order 1), a curvature crescent of size $\sim f''h^3$ per strip for midpoint and trapezoid (order 2), and $\sim f''''h^5$ per pair of strips for Simpson (order 4). the midpoint and trapezoid rules have the same order but different constants: $\frac{1}{24}$ against $\frac1{12}$, with opposite signs. the flat floor is rounding error: once the truncation error drops below about $10^{-16}$ times the size of the answer, adding strips only adds more rounded numbers, and the error stops improving (it can even grow slowly as $n$ gets large). that floor is the same one you met with step sizes in topic 02. as for $\frac1{1 + x^2}$: a more careful error analysis (the Euler–Maclaurin formula) shows that Simpson's $h^4$ error term is proportional to $f'''(b) - f'''(a)$, and for this function $f'''(0) = f'''(1) = 0$, so the $h^4$ term vanishes and the next term, $\propto h^6$, takes over. it's a preview of experiment 2: what happens at the *ends* of the interval controls the error.

## experiment 2: a function that repeats itself

**what people usually predict:** Simpson wins, as in experiment 1, by a big margin. the trapezoid rule needs hundreds of strips for 10 digits.

**what happens:** the trapezoid rule wins, by a landslide. at $n = 8$ its error is $1.3 \times 10^{-6}$ while Simpson's is $1.1 \times 10^{-2}$, ten thousand times worse. the trapezoid rule reaches 10 digits with **16** strips (its error is then exactly 0 in floating point), and Simpson needs **32**. the left-rectangle and midpoint rules do just as well as the trapezoid rule, and the left and trapezoid errors are *identical*. the trapezoid's "measured order" comes out around 15: the error isn't falling like a power of $n$ at all, but faster than any power (each doubling of $n$ roughly squares it).

**why:** for a function that repeats with period $b - a$, $f(a) = f(b)$, so the left sum $h(y_0 + \dots + y_{n-1})$ and the trapezoid sum $h(\frac{y_0}{2} + y_1 + \dots + y_{n-1} + \frac{y_n}2)$ are the same number. more importantly, the trapezoid rule's error can be written as a series of terms like $h^2(f'(b) - f'(a))$, $h^4(f'''(b) - f'''(a))$, and so on (the Euler–Maclaurin formula). for a smooth periodic function over a full period, every derivative takes the same value at both ends, so *every* one of these terms is zero, and the error shrinks faster than any power of $h$, exponentially fast in $n$. Simpson's rule doesn't share this property. the crispest way to see why: on the same grid, Simpson's rule is a combination of two trapezoid rules, $S_n = \frac{4T_n - T_{n/2}}{3}$ (check it from the weights). that combination is designed to cancel the $h^2$ error term, but here there is no $h^2$ term to cancel, so all it does is mix in $-\frac13 T_{n/2}$, the trapezoid rule with *half* the points. Simpson inherits the error of that coarser rule (divided by 3), which for an exponentially convergent rule is enormously bigger than $T_n$'s own error. the lesson is that "order of convergence" describes the worst case for smooth functions. structure in the problem (here periodicity) can make a "low-order" method far better. this is why the trapezoid rule is the method of choice for periodic integrals, and it's closely related to why the discrete Fourier transform works (Trefethen & Weideman, 2014).

## experiment 3: a function with an infinitely steep start

**what people usually predict:** Simpson stays far ahead, perhaps with all orders a little lower than before.

**what happens:** all orders collapse to about **1.5**: midpoint 1.48, trapezoid 1.49, Simpson 1.50 (left stays near 1.0). at $n = 2048$ the trapezoid error is about $2.2 \times 10^{-6}$ and Simpson's about $8.8 \times 10^{-7}$. Simpson is better by a factor of only about **2.5**, where for $e^x$ at the same $n$ it was better by a factor of more than a million.

**why:** the error formulas assume $f''$ (or $f''''$) is bounded. for $\sqrt x$ they blow up at $0$: $f''(x) = -\frac14x^{-3/2}$. away from 0 the rules are as accurate as ever, so almost all the error comes from the first strip $[0, h]$. there, the true area is $\frac23h^{3/2}$, and any rule built from a few point values gets a constant fraction of it wrong, so the error is proportional to $h^{3/2} = n^{-1.5}$, whatever the rule's nominal order. high-order rules only pay off when the function is smooth everywhere on the interval. two standard fixes: put more, smaller strips near the trouble spot ("adaptive" quadrature, which is what `scipy.integrate.quad` does), or substitute $x = t^2$, which turns $\int_0^1\sqrt x\,dx$ into $\int_0^1 2t^2\,dt$, a smooth integrand that Simpson's rule gets exactly right.
