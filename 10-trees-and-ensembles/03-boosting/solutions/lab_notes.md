## experiment 1: after the training error hits zero

**what people usually predict:** that the test error starts rising once the training error is zero ("now it can only memorize"), or at best stays flat; and that once every point is classified correctly, nothing changes.

**what happens:** the training error first reaches 0 at round **185**, where the test error is **0.149**. over the next 815 rounds the test error keeps *falling*, to **0.133** at round 1,000 (lowest 0.131). the margin curves keep moving right: the smallest training margin is $-0.44$ at round 10, $-0.035$ at round 100, $0.000$ at round 185 (the last point just crossed) and **0.034** at round 1,000, and the whole lower part of the distribution shifts right and gets steeper. the weak learners' weighted errors creep from 0.337 in round 1 to 0.458 on average over the last 100 rounds. the bound $\prod2\sqrt{\varepsilon(1 - \varepsilon)}$ lies above the training error everywhere and is still well above 0 after it hits 0. your `exponential_loss(y, f / 2)` lies exactly on it (to $3\times10^{-16}$: on paper 2's identity; the $/2$ undoes SAMME's doubled scale) and is down to 0.0043 at round 1,000, still falling: the loss AdaBoost minimizes never stops rewarding larger margins.

**why:** zero training error says every margin is positive, not that they're large. AdaBoost minimizes the exponential loss, which keeps rewarding larger margins, so it keeps concentrating weight on the points with the smallest margins (the ones nearest the spherical boundary) and adds trees that push them further onto the correct side. Schapire et al.'s bound depends on the fraction of training points with small margins and on the complexity of the weak learners, not on the number of rounds, so a better margin distribution means a better bound even as the ensemble grows: the effective complexity is controlled by the margins, not the number of trees. the weak learners' errors approach 0.5 because the reweighting makes every easy pattern a coin flip (the stage-1 property): later rounds are working on a shrinking set of hard points, and each contributes a small $\alpha$. the improvement doesn't go on forever, and it doesn't happen on every dataset: with label noise (experiment 2) AdaBoost does overfit eventually.

## experiment 2: where does the weight go?

**what people usually predict:** that the flipped points' share of the weight grows a little, to maybe 15–20%, since AdaBoost "focuses on hard points" and these are only some of them.

**what happens:** with `learning_rate=1` the flipped points (11% of the training set at this seed) carry **29%** of the weight after 500 rounds, and **22 of the 30 heaviest points** are flipped. the test error (against clean labels) is best at round 103 (0.194) and drifts up to 0.205 by round 500. with `learning_rate=0.1` the share rises more slowly (16% after 500 rounds, 19 of the 30 heaviest flipped), and the test error is still falling at round 500 (0.264): it simply needs about 10 times more rounds.

**why:** a mislabeled point sits on the wrong side of the true boundary, so every reasonable weak learner gets it wrong, round after round, and each time its weight is multiplied by $e^{\alpha}$. in loss terms, its exponential loss $e^{-yf}$ grows exponentially as the ensemble becomes confident (and correct) about the true label there, so it dominates the weighted training set and pulls later trees toward fitting the noise: that's the overfitting after round 103. the logistic loss $\ln(1 + e^{-2yf})$ grows only *linearly* in $-yf$, so a confidently wrong point costs much less, which is why gradient boosting with log loss (topic 04) and "robust" boosting variants handle label noise better. a smaller learning rate slows everything down, including the weight concentration, and gives you more rounds in which to stop early; it doesn't change where AdaBoost is heading. the heaviest points are a cheap mislabeled-example detector: in practice, inspecting the top-weighted training points after boosting finds label errors.

## experiment 3: better accuracy, worse probabilities?

**what people usually predict:** that log loss and error move together: a better classifier has better probabilities.

**what happens:** the test log loss is lowest at round **289** (0.323, test error 0.140). by round 1,000 the test error has improved to **0.133**, but the log loss has risen to **0.538**, and the expected calibration error doubled (0.050 → 0.097). the mean $|f(x)|$ on test points grows from 4.30 to 11.44.

**why:** the error rate only depends on the sign of $f$, and the signs keep improving. the probabilities depend on its size, and AdaBoost keeps inflating the size: pushing the training margins up (experiment 1) means making $|f|$ large everywhere, including at test points near the boundary where the true probability is close to 0.5. $\sigma(11)$ is 0.99998, so every mistake now costs a log loss of about 11 nats, and a few confident mistakes dominate the average. the population argument ($f \approx$ the log-odds) holds only for the minimizer of the *expected* loss; on a finite training set, AdaBoost drives the empirical exponential loss toward 0, which is overconfidence. if you need probabilities, calibrate $f$ on held-out data (Platt scaling or isotonic regression), stop by validation log loss rather than error, or use a loss with shrinkage and regularized leaves (gradient boosting, topic 04).
