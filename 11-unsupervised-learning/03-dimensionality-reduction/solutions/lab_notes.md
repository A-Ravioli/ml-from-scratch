## experiment 1: explained variance versus held-out reconstruction

**what people usually predict:** 95% of the training variance means about 95% of a new face; with every component, new faces are reconstructed perfectly (there are as many components as training faces, surely enough).

**what happens:** 95% of the training variance takes **111** components (108–113 across the 5 splits). those components capture **0.870 ± 0.012** of the held-out faces' variance (mean ± sd over 5 splits): the held-out error is 13%, not 5%. keeping all 320 components reconstructs the training faces exactly (error 0) but captures only **0.917 ± 0.008** of the held-out variance. the curves start together (k = 1: about 76% error on both) and separate steadily; the held-out curve keeps creeping down but flattens around 8–9%. in the image grid, the held-out reconstructions at $k = 10$ are generic blurred faces, at $k = 50$ and 111 the person is recognizable, and even at 320 fine detail (glasses frames, hair texture, the exact shading) is missing.

**why:** on training data, reconstruction error and discarded variance are the same quantity (on paper 2): $\sum\|x - \hat x\|^2 = (n - 1)\sum_{j>k}\lambda_j$, so the training curve is exactly $1 - $ the cumulative explained-variance ratio. the components are the directions that best fit *those* 320 photos, and with $k = 320$ they span all of them, so the error is 0 by construction (319 would suffice: the centred data have rank $n - 1$). a new photo is a new vector in 4,096 dimensions; the part of it outside the span of the training photos (new lighting, a slightly different pose, pixel noise) is unreachable by any combination of training components. projecting onto a larger subspace can't move a point further away (the subspaces are nested), so held-out error still decreases, but towards the size of that unreachable part, not 0. the photos here are of people PCA *has* seen (other photos of each person are in training). hold out whole people instead (8 of 40, 5 splits) and all components capture **0.881 ± 0.021**: the gap grows, because identity itself is now new. the practical lesson: choose $k$ by held-out performance on the task you care about, not by a training-variance threshold.

## experiment 2: how many dimensions?

**what people usually predict:** both criteria pick 5; or the reconstruction error turns up after 5 because the extra components fit noise.

**what happens:** the held-out reconstruction error falls steeply to $k = 5$ (from 331 to 12.6 per point) and then *keeps falling*, slowly (12.1, 11.6, ..., 9.1 at $k = 12$): its minimum is at the largest $k$ tried. the held-out PPCA log-likelihood rises steeply to **−45.24** at $k = 5$ and then *decreases* slowly (−45.30, −45.35, ..., −45.68 at $k = 12$): it picks **$k = 5$**. the explained variance at $k = 5$ is 0.977, with no visible elbow after it beyond the change in slope.

**why:** the PCA subspaces are nested ($V_5 \subset V_6 \subset \dots$), and projecting any point, training or not, onto a bigger subspace can only shorten its distance to the subspace. so reconstruction error is a monotone function of $k$ on any data, and can never select $k$ (it only shows a kink). PPCA is a density model: an extra column of $W$ claims that the data vary more along one particular direction than the noise level says. on the training data that's true by a little (the 6th eigenvalue of the sample covariance is larger than the noise level because of sampling fluctuation), so the training likelihood rises; on new data that direction has only noise variance, so the model's extra variance there is wasted probability mass and its noise variance (fitted on fewer leftover directions) is slightly off. the held-out likelihood pays for both. the penalty is small per component because the 6th eigenvalue is close to the noise level, which is why the curve declines gently.

## experiment 3: kernel PCA and the RBF width

**what people usually predict:** larger $\gamma$ means a more flexible kernel and better separation, monotonically; or that the separation is always on component 1.

**what happens:** plain PCA separates the rings no better than about 0.72 on any component (the best threshold on a direction through the centre). with the RBF kernel: for $\gamma$ = 0.1, 0.3 and 1, **component 3** separates the rings perfectly (1.000) while components 1 and 2 don't (about 0.70); at **$\gamma = 3$ component 1** separates them perfectly; at $\gamma = 10$ no component does (best 0.90); at $\gamma = 30$ component 3 again almost does (0.998); at $\gamma$ = 100 and 1,000 nothing exceeds 0.87, and the embedding becomes a scatter of local blobs.

**why:** for small $\gamma$, $e^{-\gamma\|x - x'\|^2} \approx 1 - \gamma\|x\|^2 - \gamma\|x'\|^2 + 2\gamma x^\top x' + \dots$: to second order, the feature space contains $x_1$, $x_2$ and $\|x\|^2$ (plus higher-order terms). after centring, the two linear features have the largest variance ($x_1$ and $x_2$ vary over $\pm 1$), so components 1 and 2 are essentially plain PCA, and the radius feature $\|x\|^2$, with less variance, comes third: that's the component that separates. at $\gamma = 3$ the kernel's length scale (about $1/\sqrt{3} \approx 0.6$) is between the rings' separation (0.7) and their size, so the dominant structure in feature space *is* "inner versus outer", and component 1 carries it. as $\gamma$ grows further, each point is similar only to its close neighbours along its own ring; the leading components then describe positions along the rings (local bumps), and the global radius information is spread over many components or lost. at the extreme, $K \to I$ and every direction is equally (un)informative. without labels you could choose $\gamma$ by the median-distance heuristic ($\gamma \approx 1/\text{median}\|x - x'\|^2$, here about 1.2, where component 3 separates the rings), by looking at how quickly the eigenvalues decay, or by a downstream criterion such as clustering stability.
