## experiment 1: monotone, but where does it end?

**what people usually predict:** random starts take a while but get there; every k-means start ends at the same, best value.

**what happens:** with the default `tol = 1e-6`, the three random-start fits stop after **7, 7 and 6** iterations, all at mean log-likelihood **−4.992**, which is the log-likelihood of a *single* Gaussian fitted to everything: the six components sit on top of each other (the middle panel shows six identical ellipses). run the same seeds with `tol = 0` for 1,500 iterations, and each curve stays flat (to the third decimal) for **about 180 to 600 iterations**, then jumps within a few dozen iterations to **−3.888** (seeds 1 and 2) or **−4.324** (seed 0). every curve is monotone. the 30 k-means-started fits reach between **−4.059** and **−3.844** within a few hundred iterations, **23 of 30** at the best value; the worst one has merged two blobs into one long ellipse and spends a component on a thin sliver.

**why:** random responsibilities are close to uniform, so after the first M-step every component's mean is (almost) the overall mean and every covariance is (almost) the overall covariance: the mixture is a single Gaussian written six times. that's a fixed point of EM, a saddle of the likelihood, and the random start sits next to it. near the saddle, the small asymmetries in the starting responsibilities fall into two kinds. most directions are stable: EM pulls those asymmetries back towards the symmetric point, so the gain per iteration *shrinks*, from about $10^{-4}$ at the start to about $5 \times 10^{-10}$ by iterations 12–20, and a "change below `tol`" rule calls it converged at iteration 7. one direction is unstable, but it grows by less than 1% per iteration, so it takes hundreds of iterations to become visible: that's the long flat stretch. once the asymmetry is large enough, the components separate rapidly. where they end depends on how they split: seed 0 found a worse local maximum. the k-means starts begin far from the saddle (components already on different blobs), so they climb at once, but they still inherit k-means's local optima: when k-means put one centre between two blobs, EM refines that into a long component covering both, and nothing in EM's local moves can split it again. monotone means "never worse than where you were", nothing more. look at the curve, restart from several starts, and keep the best.

## experiment 2: stretched, tilted clusters

**what people usually predict:** k-means worst, then spherical, diag, full; or diag nearly as good as full, since it can stretch.

**what happens:** median ARI over 5 seeds: k-means **0.019**, spherical **0.018**, diag **0.021**, full **0.990**. three of the four models cut the long clusters crosswise into three chunks; only the full-covariance mixture finds the three strips.

**why:** a spherical component is a ball, and the best way to cover three parallel strips with three balls is to slice across them, exactly what k-means does (it's the $\sigma \to 0$ limit of a spherical GMM). a diagonal covariance can stretch, but only along the coordinate axes; these strips are tilted by 34°, so an axis-aligned ellipse fits them no better than a ball. a full covariance can point its long axis along the strips. (without the tilt you'd expect diag to work, and it can represent the answer exactly; but starting from k-means's crosswise slices, the diagonal fit gets stuck in that local optimum. try it: remove the rotation in the cell.) you'd still pick spherical or diag models when $d$ is large relative to the data: a full covariance needs $d(d+1)/2$ parameters per component, and with few points per component its estimate is noisy or singular.

## experiment 3: choosing k

**what people usually predict:** BIC picks 4 both times; six points out of 406 can't matter.

**what happens:** on clean data the training log-likelihood rises from −4.76 ($k = 1$) to about −3.62 ($k = 6$) and then wiggles, the held-out log-likelihood peaks at $k = 4$ (**−3.747**), and BIC is lowest at **$k = 4$** (3,066). with the six duplicates, BIC picks **$k = 6$** (3,029), and the smallest variance in the fitted covariances is **1.0e−6**, exactly `reg_covar`: one component has collapsed onto the duplicated point. the held-out likelihood picks **$k = 5$**, whose held-out score (−3.764) is about that of the clean $k = 4$ model.

**why:** the training likelihood can only improve with more components (apart from local optima, which cause the wiggles), so it can't choose $k$. the held-out likelihood penalizes components that fit noise in the training set. the duplicates create a point where the likelihood is effectively unbounded (on paper 4): a component with mean on the point and variance at the `reg_covar` floor gives each of the six copies a log density of about $\log\frac{6}{406} - \log(2\pi) - \tfrac12\log(10^{-12}) \approx -4.2 - 1.8 + 13.8 \approx 7.8$ (weight, normalizing constant, and the $10^{-6}$ variance in both dimensions), against about $-3.7$ for an ordinary point. that's a gain of roughly $6 \times 11.5 \approx 69$ nats, worth about $140$ in BIC's first term, more than the $6 \log 406 \approx 36$ penalty for a full-covariance component. BIC is a large-sample approximation that assumes a regular, well-behaved likelihood; a degenerate component breaks it. the held-out data don't contain the duplicates, so the collapsed component earns nothing there: the held-out choice of 5 is "four real components plus one wasted on the duplicates", which predicts new data as well as the clean 4-component model. the lesson: inspect fitted components (tiny variances, tiny weights) before trusting any criterion, deduplicate, or use a larger `reg_covar`.
