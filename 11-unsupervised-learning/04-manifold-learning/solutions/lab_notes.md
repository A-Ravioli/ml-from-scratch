## experiment 1: perplexity

**what people usually predict:** a middle perplexity is best; at perplexity 2 the clusters get noisier; noise looks like a featureless blob at every setting.

**what happens:** at perplexity 2, each of the five clusters breaks into many small clumps and strings of a few points (5-NN accuracy still **0.993**, because each clump is pure), and the pure noise breaks into dozens of tight little "clusters" with empty space between them. from perplexity 10 on, the five clusters are clean (5-NN accuracy **1.000**), and they get tighter and further apart as the perplexity rises; at perplexity 100 each cluster is a dense dot. the noise at perplexity 10, 30 and 100 is one round, evenly filled cloud. the final KL for the clusters falls from **0.738** (perplexity 2) through 0.721 and 0.335 to **0.006** (perplexity 100); for the noise it's 0.890, 1.640, 1.427 and 0.688.

**why:** a perplexity of 2 asks every point to keep about two effective neighbours, so the input affinities $P$ form a sparse graph of tiny groups (pairs and triples of mutual nearest neighbours). t-SNE faithfully reproduces that graph: each little group becomes a clump, and the heavy-tailed $Q$ pushes clumps apart. in noise, random fluctuations decide who your two nearest neighbours are, and the plot turns that randomness into convincing-looking structure. with more neighbours per point, $P$ averages over the fluctuations and the noise looks like what it is. the KL values aren't comparable across perplexities: they measure the mismatch to *different* targets $P$. at perplexity 100 out of 300 points, $P$ is almost uniform within each cluster (every cluster member is a neighbour), which is easy to match exactly by collapsing each cluster: near-zero KL, and a plot with no within-cluster detail. choose the perplexity by looking at several (5–50 is the usual range), keeping only structure that survives across them and across seeds.

## experiment 2: what sizes and distances mean in a t-SNE plot

**what people usually predict:** the wide cluster looks bigger, maybe not ten times; C sits far away from A and B.

**what happens:** the spread ratio of the two clusters is **9.6** in the data and **1.01** in the plot: they look identical in size. for the three clusters, the plotted centres are A–B **38.2**, B–C **38.2** apart: the gap between B and C is **4.0** times the gap between A and B in the data, and **1.00** times in the plot. A–C is **1.85** times A–B in the plot, against 5.0 in the data, because t-SNE lays the three clusters out roughly in a line at equal spacing.

**why:** each point's bandwidth $\beta_i$ is tuned so that it has 30 effective neighbours, whatever the local density. in the tight cluster, the bandwidth is ten times smaller, and the resulting affinities among its members look exactly like those in the wide cluster: $P$ no longer contains the information that one cluster is tighter. t-SNE then has no reason to draw them differently. between clusters, $p_{ij}$ is essentially zero (no point of A has a neighbour in C or even in B at perplexity 30). the KL term for such a pair is $p_{ij}\log(p_{ij}/q_{ij}) \approx 0$ whatever $q_{ij}$ is, so large distances are almost unconstrained: they're set by the repulsion between clusters, which pushes every cluster away from every other by a similar amount. what you can read from a t-SNE plot: which points are neighbours, and which groups are distinct at the scale of the perplexity. what you can't read: cluster sizes, densities, distances between clusters, or the relative position of distant clusters. (Wattenberg et al., 2016, show many more examples.)

## experiment 3: the swiss roll three ways

**what people usually predict:** Isomap best on both measures; t-SNE close behind, since it preserves neighbourhoods; MDS worst.

**what happens:** MDS reproduces the spiral itself (rank correlation with arc length **0.191**, 10-NN preserved **0.281**). Isomap unrolls it into a flat strip with the colours in perfect order (rank correlation **1.000**, 10-NN preserved **0.826**). t-SNE keeps local neighbourhoods *best* (**0.864**) but breaks the global order (rank correlation **0.703**): the sheet is torn into pieces and folded into a U, and the blue end is broken off into separate clumps.

**why:** MDS on straight-line distances is PCA: the best *linear* projection of the roll, and the roll's largest extent is its spiral cross-section, so that's what it shows. points on neighbouring layers are close in 3-D, and nothing in Euclidean distances says otherwise. Isomap measures distances along the neighbourhood graph, which approximates distances on the sheet; since the rolled sheet is an isometric rolling of a rectangle, MDS on those distances recovers the rectangle, global order included. t-SNE matches neighbourhood probabilities. tearing the sheet costs little: only the pairs right at the tear lose their affinity, while folding and rearranging the large pieces costs almost nothing (small $p_{ij}$ for far-apart pairs, as in experiment 2). so it does the local job best and makes no promise about the global one. use Isomap-style methods (or PCA) to answer global questions ("what's the main axis of variation?"), and t-SNE to see local structure ("which points group together?").
