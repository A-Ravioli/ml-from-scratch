# forward-mode autodiff · lab notes

## experiment 1: what does a forward-mode gradient cost?

**what people usually predict:** "exact" sounds like "better at everything", so many predict the forward-mode gradient is faster than finite differences, or about the same. most expect all the gradient methods to scale linearly in $n$.

**what happens:** on a typical laptop at $n = 320$: your `grad` takes about **185 ms**, the finite-difference gradient about **3 ms** (**60× faster**), one `jvp` about **0.5 ms**, and the hand-derived gradient a few **microseconds**. both `grad` and finite differences grow like $n^2$ (slope 2 on the log-log plot); `jvp` grows like $n$. the ratio `grad / jvp` is a few hundred, roughly $n$. the one-input, $n$-output `jacobian` costs about the same as a single `jvp`. accuracy goes the other way: your `grad` matches the hand-derived gradient to about $10^{-15}$, finite differences to about $10^{-8}$.

**why:** a forward-mode gradient is $n$ passes, each a full evaluation of $f$, and here $f$ costs $O(n)$, so $O(n^2)$ in total. finite differences also make $2n$ evaluations, so the same $O(n^2)$, but each evaluation runs on a float array inside NumPy's compiled loops, while each Dual operation is a Python method call that allocates a new object. that constant factor (roughly 100×) is an implementation detail, not a law: compiled forward mode (JAX's `jvp`, C++ dual-number libraries) costs only 2–3× a float pass. what *is* a law is the factor of $n$: forward mode delivers one directional derivative per pass. it's cheap when there are few inputs (one input, any number of outputs: one pass), and hopeless for a loss with a million weights. the hand-derived gradient costs about one function evaluation for all $n$ partials. reverse mode (chapter 14) achieves that automatically, by propagating the chain rule from the output backwards, $w^\top J$ instead of $Jv$.

## experiment 2: autodiff differentiates programs, not formulas

**what people usually predict:** (a) that differentiating an approximate algorithm gives a rough, unreliable derivative; (b) that a tool which "does calculus" reports 2 at $x = 1$; (c) and (d) that $\sqrt{x^2} = |x|$ gives $\pm1$ or 0; (e) that a derivative of a derivative is just more calculus, so 1.

**what happens:** (a) the derivative of the Babylonian loop converges to $1/(2\sqrt2)$ **as fast as the values converge to $\sqrt2$**: errors $1.5 \times 10^{-1}, 7.6 \times 10^{-3}, 1.3 \times 10^{-5}, 1.8 \times 10^{-11}$, then exact to all 16 digits by $k = 5$. (b) `square` at exactly 1 has derivative **0.0**, and at $1 + 10^{-9}$ it has **2.000000**. (c) `np.sqrt(x * x)` at 0 gives **nan**. (d) at $x = 10^{-200}$ it gives **inf**, even though the function is perfectly smooth there with derivative 1. (e) the nested derivative returns **2.0**, silently, instead of 1.

**why:** autodiff applies the chain rule to the operations that actually ran. (a) the dual parts follow the differentiated recursion $\dot x_{k+1} = \tfrac12(\dot x_k + 1/x_k - a\dot x_k/x_k^2)$; at the fixed point $x = \sqrt a$ this has $\dot x = 1/(2\sqrt a)$, and since the update's sensitivity to $x$ vanishes at the fixed point (that's why Newton is quadratic), the tangent errors shrink quadratically too. (b) at exactly $x = 1$ the program returns the constant `1.0`, whose derivative is 0. the function agrees with $x^2$, but autodiff never sees the function, only the branch taken. real code has such branches in `max`, `clip`, `where` and special-case handling, and they're usually harmless because they're hit on sets of measure zero; when an optimizer *lands* on one, the gradient can silently vanish. (c) `x * x = Dual(0, 0)`, then `sqrt`'s rule divides $0$ by $2\sqrt0 = 0$. (d) is worse: $x^2 = 10^{-400}$ underflows to exactly 0 in the real part while the dual part $2 \times 10^{-200}$ survives, so `sqrt` divides a nonzero number by zero. the moral for losses: never take the square root of something that can be zero (a norm, a distance) without a safeguard, or you'll get `nan` gradients at exactly the configurations you care about (identical points, zero residuals). (e) is **perturbation confusion**: inside the inner call, `x` is the outer `Dual(1, 1)` and `y` the inner `Dual(1, 1)`, and since a `Dual` doesn't record which infinitesimal its dual part belongs to, `x + y` adds the two tangents as if they were one. the inner "derivative" is 2, and so is the answer. tagged dual numbers (as in Julia's ForwardDiff) keep perturbations apart; with an untagged `Dual` the rule is to never close over an outer Dual inside an inner derivative, and to build nested inputs explicitly, `Dual(Dual(x, 1), Dual(1, 0))`, as the capstone's `hessian` does.

## experiment 3: exact derivatives where finite differences give up

**what people usually predict:** finite differences with a sensible $h$ agree to several digits, as they did in every earlier experiment.

**what happens:** for $k = 40$ steps, the chain rule gives $F_{40}'(0.2) \approx -6.37 \times 10^9$, and your dual numbers agree to about $10^{-15}$ relative error. every finite difference is **completely wrong**, with relative error about 1: $h = 10^{-4}$ gives about $-6 \times 10^2$, $h = 10^{-6}$ about $+2 \times 10^5$ (the wrong sign), $h = 10^{-8}$ about $-2.6 \times 10^7$, $h = 10^{-10}$ about $+3.2 \times 10^7$. for small $k$ (try $k = 5$ or 10) they still agree to several digits.

**why:** in a chaotic map, nearby points separate like $e^{\lambda k}$ (here $\lambda \approx 0.56$). after 40 steps, an interval of width $2h = 2 \times 10^{-6}$ has been stretched to width about $10^4$, which means it has been folded around $[0, 1]$ many times. $F$ isn't remotely linear on $[x_0 - h, x_0 + h]$, so the difference quotient measures nothing. to be in the linear regime you'd need $h \lesssim e^{-\lambda k} \approx 10^{-10}$, but then rounding error $\varepsilon/h$ dominates. no $h$ works. dual numbers don't need linearity on any interval: they apply exact local rules at the exact trajectory, which is precisely the chain-rule product $\prod r(1 - 2x_i)$. is this derivative *useful*? it's the exact derivative of the floating-point program, and it correctly reports extreme sensitivity, but the trajectory itself is only meaningful for about $16/\log_{10}e^{\lambda} \approx 65$ steps before rounding errors swamp it. this is the same phenomenon as exploding gradients in recurrent networks (chapter 17): long products of Jacobians grow or shrink exponentially.
