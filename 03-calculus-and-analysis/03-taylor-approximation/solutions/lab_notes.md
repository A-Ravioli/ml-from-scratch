# taylor approximation · lab notes

## experiment 1: more terms, better approximation?

**what people usually predict:** for a smooth, bounded function, more Taylor terms can only help; maybe convergence is slow far from the center, but it gets there. changing the center "shouldn't matter much".

**what happens:** around $x_0 = 0$, the Taylor polynomials of $1/(1 + x^2)$ converge beautifully inside $|x| < 1$ and **diverge** outside it. at $x = 1.5$ the degree-10 polynomial gives about **$-40$** (the true value is 0.308), and the error grows like $1.5^K$ as you add terms: degree 20 is off by thousands, degree 40 by millions. the boundary is sharp, at exactly $|x| = 1$. around $x_0 = 1$, the dotted lines move to $1 \pm \sqrt2$, i.e. $[-0.41, 2.41]$: now $x = 1.5$ is inside and the polynomials converge there. $e^x$'s Taylor polynomials converge everywhere on the plotted range; adding terms only ever helps.

**why:** the Lagrange bound is $M_K|x - x_0|^{K+1}/(K + 1)!$, and for $1/(1 + x^2)$ the derivatives grow like $K!$: $|f^{(K)}(0)| = K!$ for even $K$. the factorials cancel and the bound behaves like $|x|^{K+1}$, which shrinks only for $|x| < 1$. the deeper reason comes from complex analysis: a power series converges in a disk in the complex plane, and the disk can't extend past the nearest point where the function stops being analytic. $1/(1 + z^2)$ has poles at $z = \pm i$, at distance 1 from 0 and $\sqrt2$ from 1. on the real line you see nothing: no kink, no blow-up, just a series that fails. $e^z$ has no singularities at all, so its series converges everywhere. the practical lessons: (1) local models are *local*, and "how local" can be set by features you can't see; (2) higher order isn't automatically better. the same phenomenon, polynomial approximations that blow up away from where they're anchored, returns as the Runge phenomenon in topic 07, and it's one reason ML prefers piecewise and low-order models with many local pieces over single high-degree polynomials.

## experiment 2: how far can you trust a quadratic model?

**what people usually predict:** a disk around the center (the error is "about $\|d\|^3$"), or an ellipse aligned with the Hessian. a common guess is that the region is bigger on the steep walls, since the function there "looks more like a parabola".

**what happens:** the region is strongly lopsided. at the center $(-0.5, 0.55)$, the trust radius ranges from about **0.08** to the maximum we test (**3.2**), a **40×** spread, depending on direction. it's long in directions where the model predicts a large change and the valley's curvature doesn't interfere, and short where a step would cross into the curved part of the valley. the Hessian there has eigenvalues of about $-5.6$ and $35.6$: the model is a saddle, even though $f \ge 0$ everywhere. moving the center changes the shape completely; near the valley floor the region shrinks along the valley, where the function bends away from any parabola.

**why:** the actual reduction differs from the predicted one by the Taylor remainder, roughly $\tfrac16\nabla^3f[d, d, d]$, a cubic form that's large in some directions and zero in others. the predicted reduction is $-g^\top d - \tfrac12d^\top Hd$, which is large along the gradient and along high-curvature directions. their ratio, and so $|\rho - 1|$, depends strongly on direction. a trust-region optimizer uses a ball (or an ellipse) as its region because it needs something simple to optimize the model over, and it adapts the radius from $\rho$ after each step. this experiment shows why the radius has to be adapted: no fixed radius suits every direction and every point. it also shows why the Rosenbrock valley is hard: the quadratic model is only reliable in a thin sliver along the direction of the valley.

## experiment 3: where does Newton's method go?

**what people usually predict:** Newton is an optimization method, so it goes to the minimum from most nearby starts, perhaps failing from a few unlucky ones. and every step should decrease $f$, at least once you're close.

**what happens:** exactly **50%** of the starting grid ends at the minimum $(1, 0)$ and **50%** at the saddle $(-1, 0)$: every start with $x_0 > 0$ goes to the minimum and every start with $x_0 < 0$ to the saddle. about **94 of 400** first steps *increase* $f$, and your `quadratic_model` itself predicted an increase for about **96 of 400**, nearly the same steps.

**why:** the updates decouple. in $y$, $f$ is the parabola $y^2$, and one Newton step lands exactly on $y = 0$. in $x$, the update is $x \leftarrow x - (3x^2 - 3)/(6x) = (x^2 + 1)/(2x)$, which preserves the sign of $x$: it's the Babylonian iteration for $\sqrt1$, converging to $+1$ from positive starts and $-1$ from negative ones. at $x = -1$ the cubic $x^3 - 3x$ has a local *maximum*, so in that region the Hessian is $\operatorname{diag}(6x, 2)$ with $6x < 0$: the quadratic model is a saddle, and its stationary point is a saddle of the model. Newton's method solves $\nabla f = 0$; it is a root finder applied to the gradient, and it is indifferent to whether the root is a minimum, a maximum or a saddle. the model even announces this: when $H$ is indefinite, the predicted change at the Newton step, $-\tfrac12g^\top H^{-1}g$, can be positive (on paper 4), and the steps where your model predicted an increase are almost exactly the steps where $f$ increased. the fixes are what the capstone builds: check whether the step is a descent direction, modify the Hessian when it isn't positive definite, and use a line search so every accepted step decreases $f$.
