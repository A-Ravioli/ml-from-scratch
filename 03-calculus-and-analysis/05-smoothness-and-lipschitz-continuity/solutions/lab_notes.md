# smoothness & lipschitz continuity · lab notes

## experiment 1: the knife edge at $2/L$

**what people usually predict:** after the lesson, most people predict the quadratic story: converge at $1.9/L$, diverge at $2.1/L$ and certainly at $3/L$, with the true threshold right at $2/\hat L$.

**what happens:** your sampled estimate is $\hat L \approx 2.12$ (a little below the global bound $\lambda_{\max}(X^\top X)/4n + \lambda \approx 2.62$). gradient descent converges at **every** step in the table, including $3/\hat L$ and $5/\hat L$, and the faster steps actually converge *faster*. your `largest_stable_step` finds the real threshold near **$10/\hat L$**, five times the "limit". the local smoothness at the minimum, from your `hessian_smoothness`, is about **0.41**, and $2/L_{\text{local}} \approx 10.3/\hat L$: that's the threshold that matters. (what `largest_stable_step` measures is "converges to within `tol` = $10^{-3}$ of the starting gradient in 200 steps"; the answer, about $10/\hat L$, doesn't move for other budgets and tolerances, because just above $2/L_{\text{local}}$ the iterates fall into a bounded 2-cycle around $w^*$, never blowing up but never converging either: the run *survives* without *converging*.)

**why:** the logistic loss's curvature is $\frac1n\sum_i\sigma(z_i)(1 - \sigma(z_i))x_ix_i^\top + \lambda I$, and $\sigma(1 - \sigma) \le \tfrac14$ is only attained where $z_i = y_ix_i^\top w = 0$, i.e. where the model is maximally unsure about every point at once. near the optimum most points are classified with some confidence, so their weights $\sigma(1 - \sigma)$ are much smaller than $\tfrac14$ and the local curvature is far below the global $L$. the stability of a fixed point depends on the curvature *at that point*: gradient descent converges to $w^*$ for $\eta < 2/\lambda_{\max}(H(w^*))$, as long as the transient doesn't blow up. so $2/L$ is a **guarantee** (every step decreases $f$, from anywhere), not a **prediction** of the threshold. the moral for learning-rate tuning: the global smoothness constant is a safe starting point, and you can often go well beyond it. it's also a warning: in deep learning, training pushes the local curvature up until it hovers right around $2/\eta$, the "edge of stability" (Cohen et al., 2021), a regime that classical theory doesn't cover.

## experiment 2: estimating $L$ by sampling, in high dimensions

**what people usually predict:** with 5,000 pairs, surely close; maybe 80 or 90 out of 100. many expect the estimate to improve steadily with more samples.

**what happens:** in $d = 50$, a typical random direction sees a curvature of only about **10** (median of $\|Au\|/\|u\|$), the best of 2,000 random directions about **48**, and your `smoothness_estimate` over 100 points (4,950 pairs) about **45**. gradient descent at $1.9/\hat L$ explodes: after 100 steps $f$ is around **$10^{102}$**. your `hessian_smoothness` at a single point returns **100.00**, and gradient descent at $1.9/100$ converges. lowering $d$ (slider) brings the sampled estimate up; raising it makes it worse.

**why:** the curvature along a unit direction $u$ is $\|Au\| = \big(\sum_i\lambda_i^2(q_i^\top u)^2\big)^{1/2}$. for a random direction in $d$ dimensions, $(q_1^\top u)^2 \approx 1/d$: the chance of pointing along the one sharp direction is tiny. so $\|Au\|^2 \approx (100^2 + (d - 1))/d \approx 200$ for $d = 50$, i.e. about 14, and the typical value is smaller still. the maximum over many pairs grows only slowly, because the distribution of $(q_1^\top u)^2$ has an exponentially thin tail near 1. this is the high-dimensional near-orthogonality from chapter 02 · topic 01 again: random probing almost never finds a needle. the Hessian (or power iteration on Hessian-vector products) finds the sharp direction *deliberately*, because it computes how $f$ curves in every direction at once. and the direction of the error matters: overestimating $L$ only costs speed, underestimating it costs divergence.

## experiment 3: Lipschitz but not smooth

**what people usually predict:** it converges, just more slowly; or it oscillates but the oscillations die out.

**what happens:** on $|x_1| + |x_2|$, gradient descent with step 0.15 gets to within about **0.17** of the minimum and stays there forever, bouncing. on the Huber version it converges to about $10^{-60}$. your `smoothness_estimate` with points within 0.05 of the minimum gives about **500** for $|x|$ (and it grows without bound as the sample points get closer to the axes), and exactly **10** for Huber, matching $1/\delta$.

**why:** the gradient of $|x_1| + |x_2|$ is $(\pm1, \pm1)$ right up to the axes, so every step has length $0.15\sqrt2$ regardless of how close you are, and once within a step of the minimum the iterate overshoots back and forth. the smoothness estimate blows up because two points on opposite sides of an axis, a distance $\delta x$ apart, have gradients differing by 2: slope $2/\delta x \to \infty$. there's no $L$, so the descent lemma, and with it every fixed-step guarantee, says nothing. the Huber function replaces the corner by a parabola of width $\delta$: it's $1/\delta$-smooth, the step $0.15 < 2\delta = 0.2$ is inside the stability limit, and near the minimum it's a quadratic where gradient descent converges linearly with rate $|1 - 0.15/0.1| = 0.5$. for genuinely non-smooth losses (L1 regularization, hinge loss, ReLU networks at their kinks), the remedies are decaying step sizes (subgradient methods, rate $O(1/\sqrt k)$), smoothing, or proximal methods that handle the non-smooth part exactly (chapter 08's lasso).
