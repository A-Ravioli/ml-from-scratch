## experiment 1: when Adam beats SGD, and when it doesn't

**what people usually predict:** Adam wins on both problems ("it's adaptive"), or the two tie on both; few people expect a rotation to matter, since it doesn't change the eigenvalues.

**what happens:** on the **aligned** quadratic, Adam (best lr 0.1) reaches $f - f^* \approx 6\times10^{-9}$ after 300 steps, while SGD with momentum (best lr $3\times10^{-6}$) is still at about **59**: Adam wins by ten orders of magnitude. on the **rotated** quadratic, SGD ends at about **56**, essentially the same as before, while Adam ends at about **240**, *worse* than SGD. the eigenvalues are identical in both problems.

**why:** along an axis with curvature $a_i$, the gradient coordinate is $a_iw_i$; Adam divides it by a running estimate of its magnitude, $\sqrt{\hat v_i} \approx |a_iw_i|$, so every coordinate moves at about the same speed regardless of $a_i$. on the aligned problem that's a perfect diagonal preconditioner: the condition number $10^6$ simply disappears. SGD's step is limited by the largest curvature ($\eta < 2(1 + \mu)/L$), so the flat directions crawl, even with momentum (which only improves $\kappa$ to $\sqrt\kappa = 1{,}000$, still more than 300 steps). after a rotation, every coordinate mixes stiff and flat directions, so no per-coordinate rescaling can separate them; Adam's normalization then just makes its steps noisy and badly sized. SGD is **rotation-invariant** (rotating the problem rotates its iterates, so the loss curve is the same up to rounding and the different start in the new basis), Adam isn't. the practical lesson: adaptive methods shine when badly scaled directions line up with the parameters (features in different units, embedding rows for rare and common tokens, the different layers of a deep network), and that's often the case in practice, which is why Adam is the default for transformers. but their advantage comes from that alignment, not from some general ability to handle ill-conditioning.

## experiment 2: the noise floor

**what people usually predict:** the loss keeps going down slowly, or plateaus at a level that doesn't depend much on the batch size.

**what happens:** with $\eta = 0.05$, constant-step SGD plateaus at a level that falls with the batch size: about **$1.1\times10^{-2}$** (batch 1), **$2.2\times10^{-3}$** (4), **$3.2\times10^{-4}$** (16) and **$3.4\times10^{-5}$** (64). your `sgd_noise_floor` predicts $1.06\times10^{-2}$, $2.6\times10^{-3}$, $6.5\times10^{-4}$ and $1.55\times10^{-4}$: spot on for small batches, but too pessimistic for large ones, by 2× at batch 16 and almost 5× at batch 64. when the batches of 64 are drawn *independently* instead of by reshuffling once per epoch, the measured floor is **$1.8\times10^{-4}$**, matching the prediction. the decaying schedule (batch 1) gets to about **$10^{-5}$**, a thousand times below its constant-rate floor. halving $\eta$ with the slider (to 0.025) lowers the batch-1 and batch-4 floors by a factor of about 2.3–2.5 (to $4.9\times10^{-3}$ and $8.6\times10^{-4}$), but the batch-64 run then hasn't even reached its floor after 30 epochs ($1.2\times10^{-3}$): smaller steps also mean slower progress.

**why:** the formula (on paper 3) assumes the gradient noise at each step is *independent* of the noise at every other step. sampling without replacement within an epoch breaks that assumption in a helpful way: the minibatches of one epoch partition the data, so their noises (at $w^*$) sum to exactly zero over the epoch. whatever noise one batch adds, the rest of the epoch takes back. when an epoch is long compared with the time SGD takes to forget (about $1/(\eta a_{\min}) \approx 60$ steps here), the cancellation comes too late to matter, and the independent-noise formula is accurate (batch 1: 1,000 steps per epoch). when an epoch is short (batch 64: 15 steps), the cancellation happens within SGD's memory and the iterate barely feels the noise. this is the "random reshuffling beats sampling with replacement" effect (Gürbüzbalaban, Ozdaglar & Parrilo, 2021, [arXiv:1510.08560](https://arxiv.org/abs/1510.08560); Mishchenko, Khaled & Richtárik, 2020, [arXiv:2006.05988](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.05988)), and it's why `minibatch_indices` shuffles once per epoch rather than sampling batches independently. the scaling the formula does get right is the important part: the floor is proportional to $\eta$ and (for small batches) to $1/b$. so lowering the floor costs either smaller steps or bigger batches, and a decaying learning rate gets past it entirely, at the price of slower progress later on (the Robbins–Monro trade-off). note that at equal *epochs* the comparison favours small batches: batch 1 takes 64 times as many steps as batch 64 for the same number of gradient evaluations.

## experiment 3: same training loss, different weights

**what people usually predict:** if they all reach zero training error, they found "the" solution, and test error is the same; or Adam does better because it's the more sophisticated method.

**what happens:** SGD with momentum lands *exactly* on the minimum-norm solution $X^+y$ (distance $< 10^{-2}$, and test MSE **27.9** on the sparse truth, **40.7** on the dense one). Adam and Adagrad also fit the training data (train MSE $3\times10^{-4}$ and $10^{-31}$), but land on different interpolating solutions, a distance of about **3.3–3.7** away, with larger norms (5.1 vs 4.0 sparse, 6.6 vs 5.5 dense) and much worse test error: **55** (sparse) and **58** (dense). in the scatter plots SGD's weights lie on the diagonal; Adam's are scattered around it.

**why:** the gradient of the training loss is $X^\top(Xw - y)/n$, always a combination of the rows of $X$. starting from $w = 0$, gradient descent and SGD with momentum (both linear combinations of past gradients) never leave the row space of $X$. the only interpolating solution in the row space is the minimum-norm one, so that's where they converge: an **implicit bias** towards small $\ell_2$ norm, the same kind of bias as topic 02's max-margin result. Adam and Adagrad rescale each coordinate of the gradient differently, which pushes the iterate out of the row space, so they converge to some other interpolator, determined by the trajectory rather than by any norm you'd choose on purpose. among all interpolators, the minimum-norm one is the one ridge regression approaches as $\lambda \to 0$, and here it generalizes better for both kinds of ground truth. Wilson et al. (2017) built examples where the gap is far larger, and found that adaptive methods often generalized worse than SGD on vision benchmarks. the lesson isn't "never use Adam", it's that **the optimizer is part of the model**: when the training loss has many minima (any overparameterized model, including every neural network), which one you get, and how well it generalizes, depends on the optimizer, and the training curve can't tell you.
