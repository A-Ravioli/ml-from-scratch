# constraints & duality · lab notes

## experiment 1: a support vector machine, solved through its dual

**what people usually predict:** most multipliers nonzero ("every point constrains the line a bit"), or about half; that the primal objective decreases towards the optimum from above, like a normal minimization.

**what happens:** only **2 of the 40** multipliers are nonzero ($\lambda \approx 1.56$ and $1.88$), one for a point of each class, and both points sit exactly on the margin lines $w^\top x = \pm1$. the weight vector is $w \approx (1.544, 1.026)$, with margin width $2/\|w\| \approx 1.08$, and it equals $\sum_i\lambda_iy_ix_i$ exactly. the final duality gap is about $10^{-10}$ and all four KKT residuals are below $2\times10^{-10}$. during the iterations, the dual value rises monotonically, and the primal value $f(x(\lambda_k))$ rises *too*, from below, towards the same limit.

**why:** stationarity of the Lagrangian gives $w = \sum_i\lambda_iy_ix_i$, so $w$ is built only from the points with $\lambda_i > 0$. complementary slackness says $\lambda_i > 0$ only if the constraint is tight, $y_iw^\top x_i = 1$: only points on the margin can have a price. every other point satisfies its constraint with room to spare, so its multiplier is zero and it has no influence: delete it and the classifier doesn't change; delete a support vector and the margin can widen. this is the defining property of the SVM (chapter 09), and why it's called that. the primal value starts low because $x(\lambda)$ for small $\lambda$ is *infeasible*: with no prices, the Lagrangian's minimizer is $w = 0$, which violates every constraint. weak duality only bounds $f$ at *feasible* points, so an infeasible $x(\lambda)$ can have any value; as the prices rise, $x(\lambda)$ is pushed into the feasible set and its value climbs to the optimum. the dual route therefore approaches the solution "from outside", and the gap (or the primal residual) tells you when you're close.

## experiment 2: what the constraint set does to the solution

**what people usually predict:** a few small weights for both constrained versions; "exactly zero" seems unlikely from a gradient method on a smooth loss.

**what happens:** the simplex-constrained solution has **14 of 20 weights exactly zero**; the ball-constrained one, with the same norm (0.631), has **none**, and neither does the unconstrained least-squares solution. the simplex solution keeps only 6 nonzero weights, and pays for it with a higher loss (7.77, against 6.22 for the ball and 5.95 unconstrained).

**why:** `project_simplex` subtracts a threshold and clips at zero, so every coordinate below the threshold lands *exactly* on zero, and at the optimum those coordinates sit at the boundary $w_i = 0$ with a positive multiplier (the gradient pushes them negative; the constraint holds them at zero). the ball's projection only rescales, so it never produces a zero: its boundary is smooth, with no corners or edges. the simplex is the part of the $\ell_1$ ball with $w \ge 0$ (and $\sum_iw_i = 1$), and $\ell_1$ balls have corners on the coordinate axes; the optimum of a smooth loss over such a set tends to land on a low-dimensional face, i.e. with many coordinates exactly zero. this is the geometry behind the lasso's sparsity in chapter 08, where the $\ell_1$ constraint becomes an $\ell_1$ penalty (its Lagrangian form) and the projection becomes soft-thresholding.

## experiment 3: multipliers are prices

**what people usually predict:** that loosening any constraint improves the optimum somewhat; maybe that the change roughly matches $\lambda$, without a firm idea of the sign.

**what happens:** moving a slack constraint ($\lambda_i = 0$) changes the optimum by exactly **0**. for the two active ones, the measured rate of change (a central difference with $\epsilon = 10^{-4}$) matches $-\lambda_i$ to all five printed decimals: **−9.45414** and **−0.56291**. so do the two equality constraints: **−0.12113** and **−2.88448**. (a one-sided difference, $(f^*(h + \epsilon) - f^*(h))/\epsilon$, agrees only to about $2\times10^{-4}$, e.g. −0.1209 against −0.1211.)

**why:** on paper 3 shows $\partial f^*/\partial b = -\lambda$ for equality constraints, and the same argument works for inequalities as long as the set of active constraints doesn't change, which it doesn't for a small enough $\epsilon$. a slack constraint isn't touching the optimum, so moving it a little changes nothing, consistent with its zero price. the optimal value is a smooth (piecewise quadratic) function of $h$, so a one-sided difference is off by about $\tfrac12\epsilon$ times its curvature, here about $10^{-4}$; the central difference cancels that term, and since $f^*$ is exactly quadratic in $h$ while the active set doesn't change, what remains is rounding and the dual solver's tolerance. a large $\epsilon$ breaks the prediction when the active set changes (a slack constraint becomes active, or an active one becomes slack): the price is a *local* derivative. inequality multipliers are never negative because loosening an inequality can only enlarge the feasible set, so it can't make the optimum worse; loosening an equality ($b \to b + \epsilon$) *moves* the feasible set, which can help or hurt, so its price can have either sign. this "price of a constraint" reading is how multipliers are used in practice: in an SVM, $\lambda_i$ measures how much point $i$ is holding up the margin.
