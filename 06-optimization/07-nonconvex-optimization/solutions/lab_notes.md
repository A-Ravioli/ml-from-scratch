## experiment 1: where gradient descent gets stuck on Rastrigin

**what people usually predict:** a sizeable fraction (a third, a half) of gradient-descent runs find the global minimum, since it's the deepest point; random kicks "shake it loose" and help a lot.

**what happens:** starting in $[-2, 2]^2$, gradient descent reaches the global minimum in **6%** of 100 runs, ending in **24** different local minima, with median final value **3.98** (the value at dimples two steps from the origin, like $(\pm2, 0)$; the four next to it have $f \approx 0.99$). perturbed gradient descent with kicks of radius 0.1 gives *exactly* the same result: 6%, the same 24 minima. with kicks of radius 0.6 it finds the global minimum in **34%** of runs (median best value 0.99). widening the start box with the slider makes plain gradient descent worse (the share of starts in the origin's basin shrinks like $1/R^2$).

**why:** Rastrigin's gradient field splits the plane into basins, one per dimple (roughly the unit squares around the integer points), and gradient descent simply ends in the basin it starts in: the plot shows every red cross on the nearest dimple to its start. the global minimum's basin is about $1 \times 1$ out of a $4 \times 4$ box, so about 6% is just geometry. small kicks don't help because these are genuine *minima*: all curvatures are positive (about $2 + 4\pi^2A \approx 400$), so a kick of 0.1 just rolls back. kicks only help at saddles, where some direction is downhill. a kick of 0.6 can jump over a ridge into a neighbouring basin, and since the method keeps the best point seen, it becomes a random walk between basins that remembers the lowest one: that's random search, not optimization, and its cost explodes with dimension (in $[-2, 2]^{100}$ there are about $5^{100}$ basins). this is the textbook picture of "bad local minima". the reassuring empirical fact for neural networks is that their landscapes don't look like this: most local minima found by gradient methods have similar loss (ch 19).

## experiment 2: Rosenbrock, a valley with a trap

**what people usually predict:** that Rosenbrock, the "banana valley", has one minimum in every dimension, so every run ends at $(1, \dots, 1)$; and that a path in 6-D needs more than two directions to describe.

**what happens:** of 60 L-BFGS runs from random starts in $[-2, 2]^6$, **53** reach the global minimum and **7** end in a local minimum near $(-0.987, 0.983, 0.972, 0.947, 0.899, 0.808)$ with $f \approx 3.97$ (two of those report `converged=False`: their gradient is about $10^{-8}$, at the rounding floor of topic 05, just above `tol`). the gradient-descent run from the classic start $(-1.2, 1, \dots, 1)$ also ends in the local minimum. its path is almost entirely one-dimensional: the top two PCA directions explain **100.0%** of its movement (**99.8%** + 0.2%), and on that plane it's an L: a short drop down a steep wall (direction 2), then a long crawl along the valley (direction 1) to the trap.

**why:** with $x_1 = -1$, the first two terms are $100(x_2 - 1)^2 + (1 - (-1))^2 = 100(x_2 - 1)^2 + 4$: the term $(1 - x_1)^2$ pays 4 for being on the wrong side, but moving $x_1$ towards $+1$ means going over a hump in the $x_2 - x_1^2$ term (the valley $x_2 = x_1^2$ bends up on both sides of 0), and the chain of later terms makes the hump high enough to hold a local minimum for $4 \le n \le 7$. starting at $x_1 = -1.2$ puts gradient descent on the wrong side of that hump. the path's low dimension is typical: gradient methods move almost entirely along the few directions where the gradient is large and consistent, first down the steep wall (a few big steps), then along the valley (many small ones). the 2-D picture shows that faithfully, but by construction it can't show the four other directions, along which the hump between the two minima lives: the path doesn't explore them, so neither does the plot.

## experiment 3: random slices of a high-dimensional landscape

**what people usually predict:** that the slice of 1,000-D Rastrigin looks like the 2-D one, an egg carton, perhaps even more wrinkled, since there are vastly more local minima.

**what happens:** the 2-D slice of 2-D Rastrigin shows **37** local minima on the grid. the slice of 1,000-D Rastrigin, through a random point along two random unit directions with the same span 3, is smooth and almost planar, with **0** interior local minima. (the slider shows the transition: about 5 at $n = 10$, 1 at $n = 100$.) with a span of 30 instead of 3, the 1,000-D slice starts to show structure again (about ten local minima).

**why:** a random unit direction in $n$ dimensions has coordinates of size about $1/\sqrt n \approx 0.03$. moving $\alpha \in [-3, 3]$ along it moves each coordinate by about $\pm0.1$, a fifth of a period of $\cos(2\pi x_i)$ (on paper 4). every one of the 1,000 cosine terms is nearly linear over the slice, and their sum is a smooth, gently curved surface. the egg carton is there, but its dimples are spaced about 1 apart *in each coordinate*, which along a random direction means about $\sqrt n \approx 30$ apart. the same thing happens with neural networks: slices along random directions are smoothed averages over millions of weights, so they look like clean bowls even when the landscape is rugged at the scale of single weights. that's why careful landscape studies choose and scale their directions deliberately (filter normalization, trajectory PCA, interpolation between solutions; Li et al., 2018) and why a pretty bowl is weak evidence for "nearly convex".
