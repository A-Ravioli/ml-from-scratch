# momentum & acceleration · lab notes

## experiment 1: the race

**what people usually predict:** momentum helps "a bit", maybe 2–5×; the schedule version, being the "optimal" method, finishes first or close to it.

**what happens:** to shrink the gradient by $10^8$, gradient descent takes **8,508** iterations, tuned heavy ball **384**, tuned Nesterov **389**, and Nesterov with the schedule **3,897**. the measured per-step factors for $f$ over the last 200 steps match the squares of your predicted spectral radii: **0.9979** vs $0.999^2 = 0.9980$ (gradient descent), **0.8876** vs **0.8811** (heavy ball), **0.9350** vs **0.9378** (Nesterov). heavy ball's curve first goes *up*, by more than an order of magnitude, before it plunges. the schedule's curve is ragged.

**why:** gradient descent's rate is $1 - 1/\kappa$ per step on the distance, so $\kappa\ln(1/\epsilon)$ steps: with $\kappa = 1{,}000$ and a factor of $10^8$ on the gradient, about $1{,}000 \times 18.4 \approx 18{,}000$ in the worst case; here most eigen-directions are faster than the slowest, so 8,508. tuned momentum's rate is $1 - O(1/\sqrt\kappa)$, so about $\sqrt\kappa\ln(1/\epsilon) \approx 32 \times 18.4 \approx 580$ steps; the measured 384–389 are a 22× speed-up, the $\sqrt\kappa$ story. $f$ is quadratic in the error, so it shrinks by the square of the distance rate. heavy ball's tuned step $4/(\sqrt L + \sqrt\mu)^2 \approx 3.8/L$ is far beyond gradient descent's limit $2/L$: on its own it would blow up the sharpest directions, and only the momentum term keeps them bounded (their roots are complex with modulus $\sqrt\beta < 1$). in the first few steps, before the oscillation is established, the error in the sharp directions grows: a **transient**, which is why the curve climbs before it falls. the spectral radius only describes the long run.

the schedule doesn't know $\mu$, so it's tuned for the convex worst case, where its $O(1/k^2)$ is optimal. on a strongly convex problem the momentum $\beta_k \to 1$ ends up *too large*: the slow directions are fine, but the system is underdamped and the error rings (the ragged curve), so its long-run rate is worse than the tuned constant's. "optimal" always means "optimal for a class of functions"; knowing $\mu$ puts you in a smaller class with a faster optimal method.

## experiment 2: ripples, and restarting the momentum

**what people usually predict:** that $f$ decreases every step (it's a minimizer, after all), and that a restart, which throws away the accumulated velocity, must slow things down.

**what happens:** without restarts, $f$ goes *up* on **1,258** of the 3,000 steps, in a regular pattern of dives and rebounds (the dips are where the slow coordinate crosses zero); after 3,000 steps $f - f^* = 2.4 \times 10^{-5}$. gradient descent is still at **0.27**. restarting every 200 steps gives **$1.9 \times 10^{-8}$**, a thousand times better, with an almost monotone curve. the slider shows the trade-off: restarting every 20 steps ends at **0.066** (barely better than gradient descent), every 100 at $1.4 \times 10^{-4}$, and every 300 to 1,000 steps somewhere between $10^{-11}$ and $10^{-14}$ (exactly where depends on the phase of the oscillation when each restart lands).

**why:** with the schedule, the momentum approaches 1 and the method behaves like an underdamped oscillator with almost no friction: once it has picked up speed along the slow direction, it overshoots the minimum and swings back, again and again. the amplitude decays only polynomially, so the long-run behaviour is ringing around a slowly shrinking envelope. a restart resets $\beta$ to 0, killing the velocity at a moment when it's mostly pointing the wrong way, and the first $\approx\sqrt\kappa$ steps of the schedule (where $\beta_k$ is still moderate) are exactly the "accelerated" part. restarting every $K \gtrsim \sqrt\kappa$ steps (here $\sqrt{10^4} = 100$; a few times that works best) chains those good segments into a linear rate of about $1 - O(1/\sqrt\kappa)$ without knowing $\mu$ (O'Donoghue & Candès, 2015). adaptive versions restart whenever $f$ increases, which needs no $K$ at all.

## experiment 3: optimal for quadratics, broken elsewhere

**what people usually predict:** it converges, since the function is smooth and strongly convex with $\kappa = 25$, and the parameters are "optimal"; maybe a few slow starts.

**what happens:** from $x_0 = 3.3$, tuned heavy ball (lr $1/9$, momentum $4/9$) never converges: after a few steps it locks into a **3-cycle**, $-1.802 \to 2.116 \to 0.647 \to -1.802 \to \dots$, forever. about **12%** of the starts in $[-5, 5]$ fail the same way (88% converge). tuned Nesterov converges from every start (from 3.3, in 2 steps, because its step $1/L$ exactly undoes the curvature-25 piece).

**why:** the quadratic analysis assumes the gradient is one fixed linear map, $\nabla f(x) = Ax$, so every step multiplies the error state by the same matrix, whose spectral radius is $2/3$. here the gradient is piecewise linear: relative to the minimizer at 0, the point $2.116$ has gradient $25 \cdot 2.116 - 24 = 28.9$, an "effective curvature" $28.9/2.116 \approx 13.7$, while the points on the first piece see 25. each step applies a different matrix, each with spectral radius below 1, but a *product* of stable matrices can be unstable, and heavy ball has found a sequence that returns exactly to its start. Lessard, Recht & Packard (2016) built this example to show that heavy ball has no global guarantee on smooth strongly convex functions with these parameters. Nesterov's method does, because its convergence proof uses only the inequalities that define $L$-smoothness and $\mu$-strong convexity, not a fixed matrix. the general lesson: an analysis on quadratics tells you what happens *near* a minimum where the function looks quadratic; it doesn't tell you what happens far away. that matters for neural networks, where nothing is quadratic and momentum methods are tuned by trial and error.
