# second-order methods · lab notes

## experiment 1: iterations versus seconds

**what people usually predict:** Newton fewest iterations everywhere (correct), and therefore fastest everywhere, or slowest everywhere "because Hessians are expensive"; BFGS somewhere between Newton and L-BFGS.

**what happens:** (times are from the author's laptop; yours will differ, but the shapes won't.) Newton needs **6–7 iterations at every $d$** from 10 to 400. gradient descent with a line search needs 76 to about 2,000. BFGS needs 72 to 329, and L-BFGS 22 to 100: *fewer* than full BFGS at every size. in time, Newton is fastest up to $d \approx 100$ (about 2–12 ms), but its cost per iteration grows steeply (175 ms at $d = 400$, against L-BFGS's 24 ms), and BFGS's time grows too (418 ms at $d = 400$). L-BFGS is the fastest from $d = 200$ on.

**why:** Newton's iteration count doesn't depend on the dimension or the conditioning: it sees the problem in its natural coordinates and converges quadratically once close. but each iteration builds the $d \times d$ Hessian ($O(nd^2)$ for $n$ examples) and solves a $d \times d$ system ($O(d^3)$, here with your pure-Python-loop LU from chapter 02, so the constant is large too). BFGS's iteration costs $O(d^2)$ (a matrix-vector product and a rank-two update), L-BFGS's $O(md)$, on top of the $O(nd)$ gradient that every method pays. so the trade-off is cost per iteration against number of iterations: at small $d$ a few expensive Newton steps win, at large $d$ many cheap L-BFGS steps win, and gradient descent loses on both counts because $\kappa$ inflates its iteration count. at $d = 10^5$, Newton would need a $10^5 \times 10^5$ Hessian (80 GB) and $10^{15}$ flops per solve; L-BFGS would need a few megabytes. (why full BFGS takes more iterations than L-BFGS is experiment 2's story: its one-time initial scaling, not stale curvature.)

## experiment 2: why does full BFGS lose to L-BFGS?

**what people usually predict:** BFGS wins, since it keeps all the curvature information and L-BFGS throws most of it away; or, if they've seen experiment 1, that L-BFGS wins *because* it forgets old curvature that went stale as the logistic loss's Hessian changed along the path.

**what happens:** on the 20-feature logistic loss, BFGS needs **131** iterations, L-BFGS with 10 pairs **54**, and L-BFGS that never forgets (`history_size=100000`) **37**: the method with *the same pairs as BFGS* is the fastest. the frozen-Hessian quadratic, where nothing can go stale, gives the same ranking: **90**, **49**, **34**. BFGS with an *exact* line search on that quadratic needs **21** iterations, about $d = 20$, as theory says. the right panel shows why: the first step's scale is $\gamma_0 = s^\top y/y^\top y \approx 1.44$, while the later steps have a median $\gamma$ of about **37**, 26 times larger. the clinching control: BFGS seeded with $37I$ instead of $\gamma_0I$ needs only **42** iterations on the logistic loss and **41** on the frozen quadratic, on par with L-BFGS.

**why:** "forgetting stale curvature" is ruled out twice: never forgetting helps rather than hurts, and the frozen quadratic has no stale curvature, yet BFGS still loses. what's left is the one difference between BFGS and never-forgetting L-BFGS: the starting matrix. BFGS seeds itself once with $\gamma_0I$ and then corrects it one direction per update; L-BFGS re-seeds with the newest $\gamma_kI$ at *every* iteration (check-your-understanding question 5). $\gamma_0$ comes from the very first step, a steepest-descent step that the steep directions dominate, so it's tiny: in every direction BFGS hasn't explored yet, its $H$ still says "curvature about $1/1.44$", 26 times too stiff, and its steps there are far too short. each update fixes the curvature along one step, so the bad seed lingers for many iterations. L-BFGS's fresh $\gamma_kI$ gives the unexplored directions a current, sensible scale at every step. the inexact line search adds to it: with the weak Wolfe test ($c_2 = 0.9$) the accepted steps are rough, so the pairs pin down the curvature less cleanly than exact steps would, and the classical "BFGS solves a quadratic in $d$ steps" result (which reappears with an exact line search: 21 steps) doesn't apply. the theory and the practice agree once you compare like with like: superlinear convergence is a statement about the limit, and the initial matrix and the line search decide how long it takes to get there. (practical BFGS codes often rescale more than once, or use a better line search, for exactly this reason.)

## experiment 3: how much memory does L-BFGS need?

**what people usually predict:** more memory is always better, approaching full BFGS from above; $m = 1$ is barely better than gradient descent.

**what happens:** on 300 features, L-BFGS needs **207** iterations with $m = 1$, **109** with $m = 5$, **68** with $m = 10$, **56** with $m = 20$, **48** with $m = 50$ and **48** with unlimited memory, with almost exactly one gradient evaluation per iteration (the Wolfe line search usually accepts the first trial). full BFGS needs **292**: worse than L-BFGS with any memory. gradient descent with a line search needs **2,782**.

**why:** even one pair gives a scaled direction: $\gamma I$ corrected along the last step, which already captures the local scale and the most recent curvature, so $m = 1$ beats gradient descent by more than 10×. each extra pair adds curvature along one more direction, with diminishing returns once the pairs span the directions where the curvature differs most: here going from 10 to 50 pairs saves 30% of the iterations, and beyond 50 nothing changes, since a run of about 50 steps never has more than 50 pairs. memory costs $2md$ numbers and the two-loop recursion about $4md$ flops per iteration, still small next to the $O(nd)$ gradient, so $m = 10$–$20$ is the usual compromise. full BFGS loses for experiment 2's reason, not because memory is harmful: with unlimited memory, L-BFGS is the fastest of all here. the difference is that L-BFGS rescales its starting matrix with the newest $\gamma$ at every iteration, while BFGS is stuck correcting its first, badly scaled guess.
