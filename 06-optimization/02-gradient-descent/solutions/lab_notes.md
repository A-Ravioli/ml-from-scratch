## experiment 1: the condition number and the zig-zag

**what people usually predict:** the iteration count grows with $\kappa$, maybe like $\sqrt\kappa$ or $\log\kappa$; and the exact line search, which picks the best possible step every time, is dramatically faster than a fixed step.

**what happens:** both counts grow **linearly** in $\kappa$: the log-log slopes are about **0.95** (fixed $1/L$) and **0.91** (exact line search). going from $\kappa = 10$ to $1{,}000$ takes the fixed step from **131** to **10,213** iterations and the exact line search from **72** to **4,890**. the exact line search wins, but only by a factor of about 2, at every $\kappa$. in the 2-D path plot (from $x_0 = (1, 1/\kappa)$), the exact-line-search iterates zig-zag across the valley at right angles, while the fixed step $1/L$ goes straight down the valley floor.

**why:** the exact step makes each new gradient orthogonal to the last (on paper 4), so on a narrow valley each step mostly crosses the valley and only a little of it goes along the floor. the Kantorovich bound says $f - f^*$ shrinks by at most $\big(\frac{\kappa - 1}{\kappa + 1}\big)^2 \approx 1 - 4/\kappa$ per step, and in many dimensions a random start is quickly drawn into the worst case (the iterates align with the two extreme eigenvectors, a classical result of Akaike, 1959), so the rate really is $1 - O(1/\kappa)$. the fixed step $1/L$ shrinks the coordinate along eigenvalue $\lambda_i$ by $1 - \lambda_i/L$, so $f - f^*$ by about $(1 - 1/\kappa)^2 \approx 1 - 2/\kappa$: half the exponent, so about twice the iterations. both need $\Theta(\kappa\log(1/\epsilon))$ steps. in 2-D from $(1, 1/\kappa)$ the fixed step multiplies $x_2$ by $1 - \kappa/\kappa = 0$, so it's eliminated in one step and the rest is a straight walk with factor $1 - 1/\kappa$; no zig-zag, but slow. the lesson: **choosing the step size better cannot remove the dependence on $\kappa$**. you have to change the direction (topics 03–05).

## experiment 2: measured rates against the theory

**what people usually predict:** exact line search fastest, then the best fixed step, then backtracking, then $1/L$; and the $1/L$ run matches its theorem's $0.95$.

**what happens:** the measured factors per step are **0.9025** for $1/L$, **0.8186** for both $2/(\mu + L)$ and exact line search (their curves lie on top of each other), and **0.7652** for backtracking from $\alpha_0 = 1$, the fastest of the four, with a wobbly curve.

**why:** for $1/L$, the theorem (on paper 2) holds for *every* strongly convex function and only promises $1 - 1/\kappa = 0.95$. on a quadratic, the slowest coordinate shrinks by $1 - 1/\kappa$ and $f$ is quadratic in the coordinates, so $f - f^*$ shrinks by the square, $0.9025$: theorems give guarantees, quadratics give the exact answer. the best fixed step and exact line search are tied at $\big(\frac{19}{21}\big)^2 = 0.8186$ because this start is the worst case for exact line search, where it takes exactly the step $2/(\mu + L)$ every time (the worked example's pattern). backtracking wins by *not* using a constant step: the accepted steps cycle through $0.25, 0.125, 0.0625$ (with $2/L = 0.1$). the $0.25$ steps are far beyond the stability limit for $x_2$ (they multiply it by $-4$) but shrink $x_1$ by $0.75$; the smaller steps then repair $x_2$. the product of a varying sequence of factors can be smaller than any constant factor, which is the idea behind Chebyshev step-size schedules (Young, 1953) and recent "long step" schedules for gradient descent (Altschuler & Parrilo, 2023, [arXiv:2309.09961](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.09961)). don't over-generalize: backtracking's rate depends on $\alpha_0$, $\rho$ and the start, and it has no better worst case than $1 - O(1/\kappa)$.

## experiment 3: when the minimum is at infinity

**what people usually predict:** either "gradient descent diverges, since there's no minimum", or "it converges to some $w$ and stops".

**what happens:** the loss falls like $1/k$: **$1.5\times10^{-2}$** after about 100 steps, **$2.0\times10^{-5}$** after 100,000. the weight norm grows without bound but only logarithmically: **3.1**, **5.1**, **7.4**, **9.7** at $10^2, 10^3, 10^4, 10^5$ steps, about $+2.3 = \ln 10$ per decade. `converged` is `False`: the gradient never reaches 0. the direction does settle: the angle to the maximum-margin direction $(1, 0)$ drops from about **23°** after the first step to **2.3°**, **0.84°**, **0.32°** and **0.14°**, shrinking by only a factor of about 2.5 per tenfold increase in iterations.

**why:** on separable data, scaling up any separating $w$ makes every margin $y_iw^\top x_i$ larger and every term $\log(1 + e^{-y_iw^\top x_i})$ smaller, so the infimum 0 is approached only as $\|w\| \to \infty$. gradient descent doesn't diverge in the explosive sense: the gradient shrinks as fast as the loss (each point's weight is $\sigma(-y_iw^\top x_i) \approx e^{-\text{margin}_i}$), so $w$ crawls outwards, and to keep the loss at about $1/k$ it only needs $\|w\| \approx \log k$. as $\|w\|$ grows, the points with the smallest margins dominate the gradient exponentially: $e^{-\text{margin}}$ for a point at margin 1 is vastly larger than for a point at margin 4. so in the long run the gradient is a combination of the *support vectors* only, $(1, \pm0.5)$, which pulls $w$ towards the maximum-margin direction (Soudry et al., 2018: gradient descent on separable logistic regression converges in direction to the hard-margin SVM solution). the far-away points still tilt $w$ upward, and their influence fades only polynomially in $k$, which is why the angle converges so slowly. two lessons for later chapters: an optimizer can have an **implicit bias** (it picks one of many near-optimal solutions for reasons the loss doesn't state; ch 19 returns to this for neural networks), and "the loss went to zero" says nothing about the weights converging. a small ridge penalty makes the loss strongly convex, so it has a unique finite minimizer and linear convergence; its direction is then close to, but not exactly, the max-margin one.
