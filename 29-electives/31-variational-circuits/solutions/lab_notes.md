## experiment 1: gradients from shots

**what people predict.** most people pick a small step, $h = 0.01$ or $0.001$, because that's what works for finite differences on exact functions (and what ch 02 taught). people who've thought about noise guess $h = 0.1$. almost nobody expects the parameter-shift rule to be *better* than the best finite difference; it looks like a finite difference with an absurdly large step.

**what happens.** the exact gradient is $-0.0805$. with 1,000 shots per circuit and 300 repetitions, the RMS error of the central difference grows steadily as $h$ shrinks: $0.025$ at $h = 1$ (the best), and **22.0** at $h = 0.001$, about 270 times the size of the gradient itself. with exact expectations (the dashed line) the picture is reversed: the error falls like $h^2$, reaching $10^{-8}$ at $h = 0.001$. the parameter-shift rule has RMS error **0.021**, below every finite difference.

**why.** each estimate $\hat f$ has standard deviation about $\sqrt{(1 - f^2)/N} \approx 0.03$, so the central difference has noise $\approx\frac1{h\sqrt{2N}} = 0.022/h$ (on paper 2): it's dividing a fixed amount of noise by a shrinking number. the exact curve is pure truncation error, $|f'|h^2/6$ for a sinusoid. at $h = 1$ the noise is smallest, but the estimate is biased: for a sinusoid the central difference equals $f'\cdot\frac{\sin h}h$, which is 16% too small at $h = 1$. the shift rule takes the largest useful step ($s = \frac\pi2$, where the noise $\frac1{\sin s\sqrt{2N}}$ is minimal) *and* divides by $2\sin s$ instead of $2s$, which removes the bias exactly. no choice of $h$ gets both. in practice that's the whole reason the shift rule exists: on a simulator you'd just use autodiff.

## experiment 2: a variational classifier

**what people predict.** people who've read quantum ML abstracts often expect the circuit to match or beat the SVM ("exponentially large feature space"). others expect re-uploading to fix whatever the single encoding can't, since the lesson says it adds frequencies.

**what happens.** on two noisy concentric circles (40 training points, 160 test), the RBF SVM reaches **0.925** test accuracy. the 2-qubit, 3-layer circuit reaches **0.662 ± 0.013** with the data encoded once and **0.677 ± 0.019** with re-uploading (mean ± s.e. over 3 initializations). re-uploading does fit the training set better (final training MSE 0.421 ± 0.028 against 0.494 ± 0.003), but the test gain is within the noise. the decision boundaries show why: neither circuit draws a ring; both carve out a lopsided blob around part of the inner circle.

**why.** encoded once, $f(x) = \sum_{a,b}c_{ab}\,r_a(x_1)r_b(x_2)$ with $r \in \{1, \cos, \sin\}$ (on paper 4), and the coefficients aren't free: they're the Pauli coefficients of $M = W^\dagger Z_0W$, which must square to the identity and has no identity component. the bowl you'd want, $\sin x_1 + \sin x_2 - c$, needs a constant $c$, which no traceless $M$ provides. (the coefficient argument is only a sketch: $M$ can also carry $Y$ components, which the $R_Y$ encoding turns into zero, and products like $XX$ and $ZZ$, so the reachable set is subtler than "$(XI + IX)/\sqrt2$ doesn't square to $I$". the measurement settles it: with the data encoded once, L-BFGS run for 300 iterations from 3 starts lands on the same minimum every time, training MSE 0.486 and test accuracy 0.675, the same as Adam. the model class is the limit, and depth can't add frequencies.) re-uploading enlarges the class (frequencies up to 3 in each feature), and the training loss shows it. 40 Adam steps undertrain it, though: L-BFGS for 300 iterations reaches training MSE 0.34–0.38 and test accuracy 0.727 ± 0.012 (3 starts; best 0.75). better, still 20 points short of the SVM. and the SVM's kernel, with one hyperparameter and a convex training problem, simply has the right inductive bias for smooth 2-D shapes.

the honest reading: this classifier isn't a good model for this problem, and nothing about it is quantum in a way that helps. at 2 qubits it's a small trigonometric model you could fit in closed form. that's typical of published small-scale results, and it's why topic 32's project asks *when* a quantum model could win, rather than assuming it does.

## experiment 3: barren plateaus

**what people predict.** "slowly falling" for everything is the most common guess, by analogy with vanishing gradients in deep networks. few people predict that one setting stays flat.

**what happens.** with the global observable $Z^{\otimes n}$ on a 2-layer circuit, the variance falls from $1.2\times10^{-1}$ at $n = 2$ to $8.4\times10^{-4}$ at $n = 10$, tracking $2^{-n}$. the local $Z_0Z_1$ on a circuit with $2n$ layers falls just as fast, to $5.3\times10^{-4}$. the local $Z_0Z_1$ on a 2-layer circuit stays flat, between $0.11$ and $0.15$ at every $n$ (bootstrap intervals overlap throughout).

**why.** a global observable compares the whole $n$-qubit state against a fixed pattern, and for random parameters the overlap of the circuit's output with any fixed $n$-qubit structure concentrates around its mean with fluctuations of order $2^{-n/2}$: that's the global-cost barren plateau of Cerezo et al. (2021), present even at depth 2. a deep circuit with $2n$ layers approximates a unitary 2-design, so even a local observable sees an essentially random state (topic 30's experiment 3: every small subsystem is nearly maximally mixed), and McClean et al.'s $O(2^{-n})$ bound applies. the local observable on the shallow circuit only depends on the gates in its backward light cone: in the Heisenberg picture, each layer of the CNOT ladder spreads $Z_0Z_1$ by about one qubit, so after 2 layers it sees a fixed handful of qubits, whatever $n$ is, and its gradient statistics stop depending on $n$ once $n$ exceeds that cone.

Chebyshev says a gradient of standard deviation $\sigma$ needs about $1/\sigma^2$ shots before you can even resolve its sign, so at $n = 50$ with $\sigma^2 \approx 2^{-50}$ that's $10^{15}$ shots per gradient component. the escape route has a price: a shallow circuit with a local observable is exactly the kind of computation whose output a classical computer can reproduce by simulating only the light cone, so trainability bought this way comes at the expense of any claim to quantum advantage.
