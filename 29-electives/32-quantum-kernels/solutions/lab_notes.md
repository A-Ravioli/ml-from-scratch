## experiment 1: kernel concentration

**what people predict.** most people expect kernel values to stay roughly where they are as qubits are added ("it's the same circuit, just wider"), or expect more qubits to mean a richer, better kernel. few predict an exponential collapse, and fewer that the shot noise wins by 12 qubits.

**what happens.** at scale 1, the mean off-diagonal kernel value falls from 0.275 at $n = 2$ to 0.0019 at $n = 10$ and 0.00076 at $n = 12$; its variance falls from $4.8\times10^{-2}$ to $3.2\times10^{-6}$ and $3.8\times10^{-7}$, roughly a factor of 3 per qubit. scale 0.1 is only slightly better ($2.8\times10^{-3}$ mean at $n = 12$). scale 0.03 keeps the mean at 0.037 and scale 0.01 at 0.18 at $n = 12$, and at small $n$ those two are the opposite problem: at $n = 2$, scale 0.01 gives a mean of 0.985 with variance $3.6\times10^{-4}$ (everything looks the same). with 1,000 shots per entry, the correlation between the estimated and exact off-diagonal entries at scale 1 drops from 1.00 at $n = 2$ to 0.93 at $n = 8$, **0.79** at $n = 10$ and **0.52** at $n = 12$; at scale 0.03 it's still 0.94 at $n = 12$, and at 0.01 it's 1.00.

**why.** the ZZ map at scale 1 scatters points over the $2^n$-dimensional state space so thoroughly that they behave like random states, for which $\mathbb E|\langle\phi|\psi\rangle|^2 = 2^{-n}$ (on paper 3) with fluctuations of the same order; the measured means sit within a factor of 3 of $2^{-n}$ (the ZZ states are not exactly Haar-random). a Gram matrix that's nearly the identity tells the SVM that every point is unlike every other, so it can only memorize. with shots, each entry has binomial noise of standard deviation $\sqrt{p/N}$: at $n = 12$, $\sqrt{0.00076/1000} = 8.7\times10^{-4}$, larger than the spread of the true values ($\sqrt{3.8\times10^{-7}} = 6.2\times10^{-4}$), so the estimate is mostly noise, as the correlation of 0.52 says. a smaller bandwidth moves states less, so they stay correlated, and the bandwidth you need shrinks as $n$ grows because the map's phases add up over $n$ single-qubit and $n(n-1)/2$ pair terms. the cost of the fix is the subject of experiment 3: small-bandwidth kernels are smooth and close to classical ones.

## experiment 2 (project, part 1): a dataset built for the quantum kernel

**what people predict.** most people expect the targeted ZZ kernel to win (that's what the labels are built for), but expect the *same* circuit at a nearby bandwidth to do nearly as well, and expect the CV-tuned RBF to recover at least some of the gap.

**what happens.** the geometric difference between the RBF kernel ($\gamma = 0.1$) and the ZZ kernel at scale 0.2 on these 300 MNIST inputs is $g = 40.5$: there's lots of room for a quantum advantage. on the engineered labels (which agree with the real digit labels on only 52% of the points), the ZZ kernel at scale 0.2 reaches **0.841 ± 0.016** test accuracy (5 splits of 150/150). everything else is at or below chance: the same ZZ circuit at scales 0.01, 0.02, 0.05, 0.1 and 0.4 gets 0.464, 0.457, 0.427, 0.485 and 0.457; RBF with its $\gamma$ chosen by CV 0.449 ± 0.011, the product kernel (CV) 0.447 ± 0.014, linear 0.457 ± 0.015. the ZZ kernel with its scale chosen by CV picks 0.2 on all 5 splits (0.841 ± 0.016), so a learner who tunes the quantum kernel finds the winning bandwidth.

why *below* 0.5? two controls separate the causes. predicting the training half's majority class scores 0.477 ± 0.007 (the classes are balanced over all 300 points, so the training majority is the test minority), and the same CV-tuned RBF on a random permutation of the engineered labels scores 0.475 ± 0.012. class balance therefore accounts for about half of the gap; the rest is **anti-smoothness**. a leave-one-out vote weighted by the RBF ($\gamma = 0.1$) kernel, predicting each point's label from its kernel-weighted neighbours, scores **0.357** on all 300 points: neighbours under $K_C$ systematically carry the *opposite* label. that's what the construction does: the labels live in the low-eigenvalue subspace of $K_C$ (the directions it considers roughest), so a smoother learns the wrong sign. our $g$ uses $(K_C + \lambda I)^{-1}$, a simplification of Huang et al.'s regularized geometric difference ($\sqrt{\|\sqrt{K_Q}\sqrt{K_C}(K_C + \lambda I)^{-2}\sqrt{K_C}\sqrt{K_Q}\|_\infty}$, which gives 22.2 here at $\lambda = 10^{-3}$; the pseudo-inverse version gives 302), so compare these $g$ values only with each other, not with the paper.

**why.** the labels are $\operatorname{sign}(\sqrt{K_Q}v - \text{median})$ with $v$ the top eigenvector of $\sqrt{K_Q}K_C^{-1}\sqrt{K_Q}$: by construction they're as smooth as possible according to $K_Q$ and as rough as possible according to $K_C$. that's a statement about one specific kernel matrix, and any change to the matrix destroys it. the ZZ kernel at scale 0.1 or 0.4 is a different matrix; to it, these labels are as random as they are to the RBF. so the "advantage" doesn't belong to quantum kernels, or even to this circuit, but to one (circuit, bandwidth, labels) triple. the labels are also unrelated to anything in the data a person would care about (52% agreement with the digits). this is the honest content of Huang et al.'s construction: a large $g$ shows that a quantum advantage is *possible* on these inputs, and the engineered labels show what data would have to look like to realize it.

## experiment 3 (project, part 2): real labels

**what people predict.** after experiment 2, many people expect the quantum kernel to at least match RBF on real data, perhaps winning on one dataset. people who've read the critical literature predict it loses.

**what happens.** with every hyperparameter chosen by 3-fold CV on the training half (5 splits of 150/150):

| data | ZZ (quantum) | product | RBF | linear |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| MNIST 3 vs 5 (6 PCA components) | 0.916 ± 0.009 | 0.924 ± 0.005 | **0.931 ± 0.007** | 0.893 ± 0.005 |
| California housing (8 features) | 0.724 ± 0.014 | 0.783 ± 0.010 | 0.773 ± 0.012 | **0.796 ± 0.005** |

paired over the same 5 splits, the differences from RBF are: MNIST ZZ − RBF $-0.015 \pm 0.008$ (4 of 5 splits negative), product − RBF $-0.007 \pm 0.007$, linear − RBF $-0.037 \pm 0.006$; California ZZ − RBF $-0.049 \pm 0.009$ (all 5 negative), product − RBF $+0.009 \pm 0.006$, linear − RBF $+0.023 \pm 0.012$. so on MNIST the ZZ kernel is slightly behind RBF, a gap of about two standard errors that 5 splits can't firmly establish, and not distinguishable from the product kernel; on California housing it is clearly worst, 5 points below RBF and 7 below linear. CV picks the ZZ map's bandwidth at 0.05 on every MNIST split and 0.02–0.1 on California housing, the low end of the grid, where experiment 1 shows the kernel is far from concentrated. the ZZ kernel's best alignment with the MNIST labels (0.572, at scale 0.02; 0.450 at the 0.05 that CV picks) is the *highest* of all four families, even though its accuracy is not.

**why.** the classical kernels have the right inductive bias for these data: smooth functions of a few informative directions (for MNIST, the first principal components separate 3s from 5s well, which is why even linear gets 0.89; for housing, median income alone is a strong linear predictor, which is why linear wins). the ZZ kernel's notion of similarity comes from the parity-weighted phases $(\pi - z_i)(\pi - z_j)$, which have nothing to do with these tasks, and CV responds by turning the bandwidth down until the kernel is as smooth, and as unremarkable, as possible. the alignment result is a reminder that alignment is a heuristic: a near-constant kernel at tiny bandwidth, after centering, looks like a quadratic-ish kernel that correlates with the labels, but the SVM with $C = 1$ doesn't exploit it as well as the RBF.

**the project's answer.** a quantum kernel can beat classical kernels only on data whose labels line up with the specific geometry of its feature map, which you can check in advance with the geometric difference ($g \gg 1$ is necessary) but only confirm by experiment. we could construct such data (experiment 2), but the advantage vanished when the bandwidth changed by a factor of 2, and on two real datasets the quantum kernel lost to standard classical kernels tuned the same way. at simulable sizes (6–8 qubits here) the comparison is between two classical computations anyway; at sizes where the ZZ kernel might be classically hard, experiment 1 says it would be exponentially concentrated unless the bandwidth is shrunk, which pushes it back towards classical behaviour. that matches the current state of the literature: no convincing quantum-kernel advantage on natural data, and rigorous advantages only for problems built from cryptographic structure.
